this frame

Frame ms is the main thread: everything from the top of the animation callback to the bottom of render(). The number this spike is about is what is not in it -- there is no per-instance CPU work at all, so this figure does not move when you push density to 3×.

the grass

against /v2's carpet

The right column is src/v2/render/grass.js as measured by scripts/check-grass.mjs -- a real scatter over real terrain out to 70 m, not an estimate. The row that decides whether villages and animals fit is the last one: 0.82 ms of main thread at 72 Hz is 6% of the frame spent before a triangle is drawn, and it grows with density. Here density is a uniform.

the ground

Eight concentric clipmap levels, each 56 cells across at twice the cell size of the one inside it, each snapped to its own grid. This is here so the grass has hills to stand on -- /v2 has a proper quadtree mesher and this is not a proposal to replace it. What is the proposal is the material: past 72 m every blade you can see is painted by it.

what BOTW actually did

Not a shader instead of polygons -- alpha-cut quads, same as ours. Three things made them cheap. One: the blades stop at 15-25 m and there is no grass geometry past it. Two: the terrain material carries everything beyond that radius, and it reads as lush because at 200 m a real meadow is also only colour and ripple. Three: placement is derived from position on the GPU, so it is never stored, streamed or visited by a CPU. Turn ripple off and watch a hillside become a golf course: motion is what sells the far field, not texture.

and why not shell texturing

Turn shells on, put your head near the ground, and read the frame time against a triangle count that barely moved. Shells draw the ground N times with a rising alpha cutoff -- gorgeous fur, and three things wrong for a Quest. Overdraw is the technique, so it costs the same at 400 m as at 4 m. Alpha test in front of a tile renderer's hidden-surface removal means every layer shades. And a stack of horizontal slices has no silhouette from a standing eye height, which is the only camera a VR player has.
