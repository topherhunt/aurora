# 23 -- Rock generator

Procedural rocks: `src/props/rock.js`, bench `gen-rock.html`, tile cut by `tools/props/cut-rock.mjs`. The scatter that places them is `src/v2/render/rocks.js`. Gate: `scripts/check-rocks.mjs`.

A rock is the opposite of a fern in every way that matters to a generator. A fern is a rosette of flat cards whose entire silhouette lives in an alpha channel; a rock is an OPAQUE CLOSED LUMP whose silhouette is the geometry and nothing else. There is no texture trick available: you cannot photograph your way out of a boulder. So the whole job is to spend a handful of triangles on a shape that reads as stone, and the answer has three parts.

## 1. Displacement is a pure function of the original unit direction

Every vertex of every base solid starts on the unit sphere, and its final position is decided by feeding that direction into `shapeRadius`, which is deterministic and stateless. Nothing depends on which solid we started from, how many vertices it had, or what order they came in.

The consequence is the one the whole LOD ladder rests on: an icosahedron (20 faces) and its subdivisions (80 at detail 1, 320 at detail 3) all sample THE SAME ROCK, coarser or finer. (Three's polyhedron subdivision splits each edge into `detail + 1`, so counts go as `20 * (detail + 1)^2` rather than doubling.) A tier change is a silhouette that simplifies rather than a different rock that pops -- and "some boulder shapes also work as medium and small rocks, just bump LOD0 -> LOD1" is literally true: every rock ships all three tiers, and only the DISTANCES at which it steps depend on how big it is.

Purity gets the tiers onto the same shape; it does not get them to the same SIZE. Sampling a lump on 20 directions loses every peak between the samples, so a raw T20 comes out smaller than the T320 it replaces even though both describe the same rock. So every tier is measured against one dense reference and given a single uniform gain matching its mean silhouette radius to the reference's. That is the number a player perceives at an LOD switch, and driving it to zero is what makes the switch invisible.

This is `buildBoulder`'s one-line lesson in `props/shapes.js` ("jitter must be a pure function of the ORIGINAL position") taken seriously. There the reason was to stop a non-indexed solid tearing apart at its seams. Here it is that plus the entire LOD story.

## 2. Flat faces come from radial plane clipping, not from noise

Noise alone gives you a potato. What makes stone read as stone at 20 triangles is FLAT FACETS meeting at hard edges -- conchoidal fracture, joint faces, bedding planes. So after displacement each vertex is clipped against a small set of half-spaces, radially from the shard's own centre:

```
r_max = min over planes with dot(p̂, n_i) > 0 of  c_i / dot(p̂, n_i)
```

Radial clipping (rather than the obvious "project any vertex outside the plane onto it") is chosen for three properties: it is ORDER-INDEPENDENT, so five planes give the same solid in any sequence; it NEVER INVERTS a triangle, because every vertex only moves inward along its own ray; and it needs no iteration to converge, because the min is exact.

`cutBias` steers the plane normals between two real rock behaviours. **Note the inversion:** a BEDDING plane is a horizontal FACE, which is a VERTICAL normal. So `-1` tilts normals toward vertical, making horizontal cut faces (bedding -- the stacked slabs of a riverbed), and `+1` tilts them toward horizontal, making vertical cut faces (columnar jointing -- the sheer sides of a crag).

A face whose three vertices were all pinned by the SAME plane is a genuine flat facet, and it is shaded flat regardless of `smooth`. Without that test, turning smoothing up to soften the lumps also rounds the fracture faces away, and the rock goes back to being a potato.

**Plane offsets are measured against the TAPERED ellipsoid, not the bare one**, and on a spire that is the difference between cuts and no cuts. A plane's offset is a distance from the shard's centre; take it from the full semi-axes and it lands outside a crown narrowed to a fifth of them, so the near-vertical columnar planes meant to make the top toothy sail past it and the tip comes out a smooth cone. `cutPlanes` finds where the normal touches the untapered ellipsoid, reads the profile at that height, and re-measures the support against the semi-axes the rock actually has there. Identical to the naive form wherever `taper` and `foot` are 0.

## 3. One tile, tinted per instance, projected per face, scaled by the rock

Every rock wears `LAYER.ROCK` -- one 128px granite speckle. Variety comes from a per-instance colour via `BatchedMesh.setColorAt`, which defines `USE_BATCHING_COLOR -> USE_COLOR` in the fragment prefix and multiplies `diffuseColor` by it WITHOUT needing `material.vertexColors` (`props/scatter.js` already tints stands this way). So granite, basalt, sandstone, wet shale and lichen-green are one draw call. That is also why the tile is graded bright and near-neutral: a tint is a multiply, so the tile's mean is the ceiling, and its hue would otherwise fight every tint laid over it.

UVs are a per-face planar projection off whichever world axis the face normal dominates -- no unwrapping, no seams to author.

**What the projection is divided by scales WITH the rock.** A fixed 0.9 world metres held absolute crystal size constant across a library spanning two orders of magnitude -- a 12 cm cobble and a 14 m outcrop wearing literally the same speckle. That is the textbook answer and it looks wrong: a 7 m crag under a 0.9 m tile is eight repeats of one photograph across its face, and eight repeats of anything reads as patterned fabric rather than stone. The tile's features sit far below the scale its silhouette promises, so the rock stops having a size of its own and starts looking like a scale model.

So `texRepeat` is how many times the tile covers the rock's largest horizontal extent, whatever that extent is in metres. That makes a shape one picture at 0.2 m and at 14 m, and makes "author the shapes at 2 m and scale them to fit the scenery" literally true. The cost is real: two rocks of different sizes side by side no longer agree about how big a grain of this granite is, so a boulder is no longer evidence about the cobble next to it. `texJitter` then rolls the repeat +/-50% per seed -- that buys back the variety constant density used to get free from rocks being different sizes, and it stops a bed of same-size cobbles looking stamped. It is rolled off `seed` alone, so it is the same on every tier: a texture that rescaled at an LOD switch would be a worse pop than the silhouette one the support gain exists to kill.

**Attributes** are always `{ position, normal, uvProj, texLayer }`, indexed with an identity index -- the shared prop material's layout (`src/material.js`), which `BatchedMesh` rejects a geometry for disagreeing with. Unlike `buildFern` there is no second `uv` layout, because `gen-rock.html` renders the real material rather than a stand-in: a rock needs the tint path to be worth looking at.

## 4. Front faces only, and a grit layer for the lighting

A rock is a CLOSED SOLID, alone among the things drawn with `createPropMaterial` -- every other one is cutout foliage, where both sides of a leaf are the same leaf and `DoubleSide` is the point. So `v2/render/rocks.js` asks for `side: THREE.FrontSide` and halves the raster work for nothing given up. The material's `normal *= faceDirection` patch, which exists to undo three's flip for leaves, is a no-op under `FrontSide`: a back face is never shaded. `check-rocks.mjs` proves the winding is outward by signed volume on every tier, the six-face hull included -- the failure mode is invisible from any angle that still has a front face to look at, so it has to be asserted rather than looked at.

**`bump: true`** perturbs the normal from a height field, so a boulder re-reads as the sun goes round instead of being a faceted blob at every hour. Two extra atlas fetches a pixel, which on a fill-bound headset is the entire cost and the reason it is a compile flag: nothing but rocks carries the block, and `setPropBump(0)` skips it on a uniform branch. The neighbours are sampled one SCREEN DERIVATIVE away rather than one texel, so the sample spacing widens with distance exactly as the mip level does and the grain flattens out instead of sparkling. The perturbation happens BEFORE the snow and moss masks, so both settle in the grooves rather than lying over a surface that only looks grooved.

**The height field is its own layer** (`LAYER.ROCK_BUMP`), generated grey fractal noise, not the granite photograph's luminance. Reading the albedo makes TONE into RELIEF, which is wrong wherever a stone's colour is not its shape -- pale veins come out as ridges, damp patches as pits -- and it looked it. A layer of its own costs 64 KB in an atlas that was already allocated, and buys a field that is actually a field. It is tiled SEPARATELY from the albedo (`setPropBumpTile`, `/gen-rock`'s `bumpScale`) because the grain that catches a low sun is finer than the tile sized to read as rock at arm's length, and welding the two makes every change to one a change to the other. It is read through a derivative, which is why `check-rocks.mjs` holds it to a tighter wrap-seam bar than the photographs: a seam in an albedo is a faint line, a seam in a height field read this way is a bright rule of wrongly-lit pixels drawn across the rock every repeat.

## The tier ladder

`ROCK_TIERS` is T320 / T80 / T20 / T6, coarsest last, and it is DERIVED rather than typed: `rockTier(detail)` takes a subdivision level and returns the name and the face count together (`rockTier(0, 'five')` is the last rung, the five-vertex hull below, and the only one that is not a sampled solid) (`20 * (detail + 1)^2`, because three splits each edge into `detail + 1` rather than doubling), so the table cannot say T320 while building 80. `faces` is per SHARD -- a 3-shard outcrop at T80 is 240 triangles -- which is why `shards` is the most expensive slider on the bench and the budget panel prints the product.

**`detail` is the dial a finer LOD0 gets chosen on.** `tier` names a rung of the shipping ladder; `detail` names a raw resolution and overrides `tier` when it is a number, which is how `/gen-rock` looks at rungs the ladder does not carry yet (up to `ROCK_MAX_DETAIL`). It is null on every path but the bench, so the world reads the ladder and nothing else, and the bench's LOD-ladder view passes `detail: null` so those rungs stay the shipping ladder. The measurement reference follows it: `REFERENCE_DETAIL` is derived from the ladder as `max(3, ...)` over the rungs, so promoting a finer tier into `ROCK_TIERS` re-measures every tier of a shape together, which is the only way `measured` stays ONE BOX PER SHAPE.

**Every rock ships the whole ladder, whatever size it is.** There used to be a fourth tier and a per-size-class ladder deciding which three of the four you got (crag 180/80/20, boulder 80/20/8, cobble 20/8), on the argument that a small rock is never seen from far enough away to need the fine end. That argument is about DISTANCE and it was being answered with GEOMETRY: the ladder decided which meshes a shape owned, while a hand-typed table of metres per BED decided when to step between them, and the two halves had no way to agree -- a rock could sit at its coarsest tier from 40 m and dissolve out of the world at 45 without the ladder having anything to do with it. One ladder plus one rule for when it steps puts the size argument in the thresholds, where it belongs. T320 is still for the big ones, and now says so in metres: at 4 m per metre of size, a 40 cm chip carries 320 faces for its first 1.6 m and a 10 m cap carries them for 40.

**T6 is the far tier, and it is a fitted hull, not a sampled solid** (`fiveHull`). An octahedron displaced by the same field keeps the rock's proportions and its lean and nothing else -- a generic diamond -- and a photographed card, tried after it, could not be made to match the mesh's light, colour, dimensions or burial from every eye. So the last rung is five vertices placed IN THE MEASURED BOX: two floor corners on one diagonal, two shoulders part-way up on the other, pulled in by `a` so the plan has the reference's own plan area, and one crown over the reference's highest point, the shoulders raised to `s` so the side silhouette has the reference's area on each horizontal axis. Its box is exactly `measured`, so a rock swapping down to it neither swells nor shrinks nor moves; the six faces are the convex bipyramid over three of the five (all ten pairings tried, the one every vertex lies inside kept), so `FrontSide` culls the right half; the shell normals are the box's ellipsoid, so `smooth` rounds its lighting as it rounds the sampled tiers'; and the floor corners hang the same `skirt` on the same terms, so a seated cap still plugs into a hill. What six faces cannot give is roundness: a rock the bed has sunk to its shoulders shows a wedge where the mesh shows a dome, and `check-rock-far.mjs` measures how much that costs (see `25-rock-scatter.md`).

### `ROCK_LOD_AT = [4, 7.5, 25]`

Metres of camera distance PER METRE of the rock's own size: T320 inside the first, T80 inside the second, T20 inside the third, the six-face hull T6 beyond. Per metre because what decides whether a triangle is worth drawing is ANGULAR size. A 2 m rock holds its finest mesh to 8 m, its second to 15, its third to 50, and is the hull past that; a 4 m one doubles all four; a 12 m tor holds T320 to 48 m and is the hull at 300. One system for every rock -- they all step at the same apparent size, only the metres differ.

Deliberately tighter than a pixel-error argument would give: sizing the step so a triangle never falls under about three pixels wants roughly 70 m per metre before the hull, and this asks for 25. The ladder is set to buy back triangles, not to be invisible, and this is the number to move if a band looks wrong -- nothing else in the system encodes a distance. `ROCK_LOD_HYSTERESIS` beside it (0.12) is how much further out a rock leaves a rung than it entered it; `rocks.js` still carries the same value as its local `LOD_HYSTERESIS`, so the two must move together.

**Judging a band is `/gen-rock`'s *world LOD* view**: one rock stepped down this ladder by the camera's distance under the rule `rocks.js` applies per instance, hysteresis included, with the distance, the live rung and this rock's thresholds on screen, and each ladder row a button that parks the camera just past its boundary. *Tier colours* dresses the four rungs red / amber / green / blue in any view so the rung on screen is never in doubt. The bench cuts hard where the world cross-dissolves, so a pop seen there is the worst case. The ladder table's metres are always this rock's, and it names the ladder size and the `×n` it multiplied them from, since two rocks' ladders can only be compared through that size.

### `rockLodSize` is the LONGEST AXIS of the box

Height alone is what you would reach for -- it is how anyone describes a rock -- but this bank is mostly flat things. A `lip` is 7 m across and 1.7 m tall, a `shingle` 1.2 m across and 11 cm tall; keyed on height the shingle would be a billboard at 2.7 m, close enough to step on. Width alone has the opposite fault: a `spire` is 2.5 m across and 6 m tall and would card while it still filled a third of the screen.

The longest of the three has neither fault and needs no weighting. It is also the RIGHT quantity rather than a compromise: the ladder is asking "how many pixels does this rock subtend", the answer is its largest apparent extent over its distance, and the largest extent a box can present to any camera is its longest axis. The billboard makes it matter -- the card spins to face the eye, so whatever it is sized to is what the rock looks like from every bearing at once. Depth is in with width because the bank elongates in plan (`elongate` defaults to 1.25 and goes past 2), so for a rock lying across the view the long horizontal axis is as often z as x. It is a CEILING on apparent size, conservative in the safe direction: a rock is never judged smaller than it can look, and the worst case is a slab seen exactly edge-on carrying a finer mesh than it needs.

## Shape dials worth the note

**`profileRadial` is the one part of the shape that does NOT go through `shapeRadius`**, because it scales the two HORIZONTAL semi-axes and leaves the vertical one alone. The first cut was one line inside `shapeRadius` -- `r *= 1 - taper * 0.45 * dy` -- an ISOTROPIC multiply, and it cannot make a spire: a vertex's height is `dy * ay * r`, so shrinking `r` near the top shrinks the HEIGHT there too, and past `taper` ~0.5 the tallest point stops being the apex and the profile folds into a dome. Being linear and symmetric in `dy`, it also flared the base into a point at the bed plane instead of sitting on it. Scaling only x and z fixes both: height stays monotone in `dy`, so the apex is the apex, and `foot` flares the bottom half INDEPENDENTLY, making "pointed at the top" and "solid at the bottom" two dials instead of two ends of one.

`taperPow` distributes the narrowing: 1 is the old linear cone; above 1 the rock keeps its width then loses it fast near the crown (the shoulders-and-a-tooth profile a weathered pinnacle has); below 1 it narrows immediately and reads as a needle. A negative `taper` narrows the BASE -- the glacial erratic and the mushroom, the same curve mirrored, so `taperPow` means the same thing. `foot` is a batter, not a skirt: quadratic in distance below the crown, so most of the flare is in the bottom third and it has faded by the top. It must reach well above the bed plane because `sit` flattens the bottom `sit` of the height onto that plane -- a flare peaking only at `t = 0` would be entirely inside the clamped part, and the dial would look broken at exactly the settings that want it most. The 0.94 clamps stop a horizontal semi-axis reaching zero, where the clip would divide by a degenerate radius; the 0.04 floor is the same guard for the product.

**`vertexJitter` and `maxAngle` are a pair.** `lumps` and `grain` move a vertex ALONG ITS OWN DIRECTION, and a radial field, however hard it is driven, leaves an icosphere's geodesic triangles exactly as even as it found them: the silhouette wobbles and the tessellation still reads as a ball someone dented. `vertexJitter` adds a three-channel noise VECTOR instead -- a domain warp, dragging vertices sideways -- which is the part that makes the mesh look broken rather than moulded. It stays a pure function of the unit direction like everything else, so the tiers still agree, and its frequency is set against the FINEST tier's vertex spacing (0.28 rad at T320): too low and it is a second `lumps`, too high and each tier resolves a different rock.

`maxAngle` is the rail that keeps that round. A vertex may not stand further off the mean of its four angular neighbours than `tan(maxAngle)` times their spacing, which is exactly "no face may jut at more than this angle". Two decisions in it. It is measured ALONG THE SURFACE NORMAL, because a vertex slid sideways is not a jut -- it is the uneven triangle `vertexJitter` was asked for -- and spending the budget on it would leave nothing for the spikes. And the neighbourhood offset is a CONSTANT 0.25 rad rather than the tier's own spacing, because a limit that read the vertex count would round a T20 harder than a T320 and the two would stop being the same rock. It costs four extra field evaluations a vertex at build time and none at runtime. `ANGLE_OFF = 60` is measured, not chosen: with every roughness slider on the bench at its ceiling at once, the steepest vertex over 40 seeds juts 44 degrees, so above 60 the rail cannot bind and the samples are skipped -- which is also why the bench's slider stops there. Cut faces are exempt; a fracture is meant to be sharp.

**Shards.** An outcrop is not one lump, it is a main mass with smaller ones crowding its skirt -- cheaper and better than making one solid do it, because the creases where two shards interpenetrate are free silhouette detail.

**`sit`.** A rock resting exactly on its lowest point looks placed; real ones are bedded in, so `sit` cuts the bottom off and stands the cut face on `y = 0`. **The shipping boulder leaves it at 0** and takes its bedding from the scatter's burial roll instead, which sinks every instance by at least a tenth of what it stands, a third if the quarter turn stood it on its long axis, and up to nine tenths (§25); the geometry's origin lands on the lowest point either way, which is the BED FACE the scatter seats against. Where `sit` is turned up it leaves a real horizontal disc down there -- triangles facing straight down at ground level, never once seen -- and that disc stays: **the rock ships closed**. Throwing those faces away would buy about a tenth of a T80's faces at `sit` 0.3 and half at 0.6, and the price is a shape that can only ever be looked at from above the ground it was bedded into. One closed solid is placed by six beds at every burial from a tenth to nine tenths, on flat ground and on a 72-degree face, so there is no orientation the world can promise it.

## Matching a tier to its reference

`meanSupport` gain: sampling a lumpy shape on 20 directions instead of 320 does not just round the corners off, it loses AREA -- every peak between two samples is cut away -- so an untouched T20 reads smaller than the T320 it replaces and the swap looks like the rock jumping backwards. One uniform gain about the bed origin buys that back (proportions, lean and the bed plane untouched) and drives the mean signed error between a tier and the reference to roughly zero, which is what a player perceives as a pop. What it cannot fix is the SPREAD: the coarse tier is still short in some directions and now long in others. That is the right trade -- a silhouette that is the right size and the wrong shape is invisible at the range a T20 is drawn at; one that is the right shape and the wrong size is a visible jump.

**`BOX_MARGIN = 1.8`**, how far outside the reference's bounding box a tier may reach once scaled up. Deliberately loose. The temptation is to pin it near 1.0 so `size` stays literally true of every tier, but that trades the error nobody can see for the one everybody can: a coarse solid's vertices sit ON the shape while everything between them is cut away, so holding its box to the reference's leaves it 13% short in apparent radius -- a visible shrink at the LOD switch -- to save an overhang that is a fraction of a pixel at the distance a coarse tier is drawn.

1.8 because **a cap that binds is a bias generator**. The gain is one uniform number and the cap is taken on the worst axis, so a spire whose T20 bulges in x and under-samples in y gets its gain throttled by x and comes out 22% short in y -- exactly the visible shrink the gain exists to remove, reintroduced by its own safety rail. Measured over 60 seeds x 9 shapes x the three coarse tiers there were then (the 8-face one included): at 1.3 that happened to 46 of 1620 builds, worst 29% off; at 1.8 it is 0 of 1620, worst 2.3%. The cap now catches only a genuinely degenerate build, which is all it was ever for. Collision and spacing read `measured`, which is the reference's box, not a tier's.
