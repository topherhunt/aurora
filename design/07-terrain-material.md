## 7. Terrain material and transparency

> **Covers:** the ground shader -- splat blending, the palette, speckle, snow line, transparency policy.
> **Read this when:** touching `src/material.js` or terrain colour.

### Splat blending: 4 layers, 4 channels

| Layer | Placement rule |
| --- | --- |
| **Snow** | Elevation above snowline, plus noise, plus weather accumulation (§10). Reduced on steep slopes -- snow does not cling to cliffs |
| **Rock** | Slope above threshold. Dominant on cliffs and gorge walls |
| **Grass** | Low elevation + moisture. Warmer valleys |
| **Packed dirt** | Path and village masks (§6) |

Weights pack into a single RGBA texture -- four layers, four channels, exactly. Two implementation notes that matter for how it looks:

- **Height-blend, do not linear-lerp.** Give each layer a height/roughness map and blend by `max(weight + height)` rather than a weighted average. This is the difference between snow *settling into* rock crevices and snow *fading uniformly* over rock. It is a few lines of shader and it is the single biggest quality lever on the terrain.
- **Triplanar on steep slopes only.** Lerp toward triplanar projection as slope increases, rather than applying it everywhere. Avoids stretched cliff textures at 1/3 the average cost.

The terrain uses its own material with small tiling textures. This is a deliberate exception to the one-material rule and costs one draw call family. It is *not* an exception to the one-ATLAS rule: the array is `RepeatWrapping`, so any layer authored to tile can be sampled from the terrain too, and the rock surface already does (below).

### Interim: two baked detail tiles (`src/terrain/terrain-material.js`, `src/terrain/grit-texture.js`)

Until the splat textures exist, the surface gets its grain from a `MeshLambertMaterial` patched through `onBeforeCompile`: a brightness speckle on everything, then dirt and moss mixes gated on `vColor.g > max(vColor.r, vColor.b)` so only vegetated ground gets them. It fades out between 12 m and 95 m, because past that it is per-pixel noise nobody asked for.

**The fields are BAKED, and that was the single largest performance decision in the renderer.** They used to be an integer-hash value noise evaluated live -- about twenty `auroraNoise` calls on a near-field fragment, four hashes each, three integer multiplies a hash, so ~240 integer multiplies per fragment on an Adreno where integer multiply is slower than the floats it replaced. The measurement that forced the change, taken on device with a runtime toggle: terrain hidden, **85 fps**; terrain drawn with stock Lambert on it, **85 fps**; terrain drawn with this file's patch on it, **30 fps**. Same meshes, same triangles, same draw calls, same fog, same lights. The terrain was never triangle bound, and no amount of LOD or chunking work could have touched it.

So the noise is gone and two 256 px RGBA `DataTexture`s carry its output. Four channels each, four fetches worst case:

| Fetch | Tile | Gate | Carries |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **macro, coarse** | 1024 m | none -- every fragment in the world | `.a` snow-line wander, `.r` regional value and tint |
| **macro, fine** | 137 m, rotated ~37° | haze | `.r` mid-range mottle, `.g` mineral staining on rock |
| **grit, coarse** | 11.7 m | `FADE_FAR` 95 m | `.r` grain and the dirt/moss mixes, `.gb` slope |
| **grit, fine** | 2.3 m, rotated | `MICRO_FAR` 40 m | `.r` fleck tint, `.a` snow sparkle, `.gb` slope |

Past 40 m that is three fetches, past 95 m two, and past the haze gate one. Both textures are `NoColorSpace` and that is load-bearing: every channel is a field -- a weight or a packed derivative -- not a colour, and tagging them sRGB would bend the value channels through gamma and recentre the gradient channels off 0.5, which looks like nothing worse than a mis-tuned shader.

Three things had to be got right for a tile to stand in for world-space noise:

- **Rank equalisation, and the retune it forces.** An fbm is bell-shaped -- raw, the field ran p1 0.26 / p50 0.48 / p99 0.78 -- so `equalise()` sorts the grid and reassigns `rank / (N-1)`, making the field *exactly* uniform and every threshold mean exactly what it says: `smoothstep(0.93, 1.0, x)` is the top 7% of texels by construction. **That is also the trap.** Value noise is centrally concentrated too -- a two-octave sum ran p01 0.16 / p99 0.84 -- and every threshold in the shader had been tuned against *that*, out in a thin tail. Swapping in a uniform field left the numbers unchanged and tripled what they caught: 12.5% -> 21.0% on the region tint, 7.6% -> 24.0% on the mottle's dry end, 0.33% -> 3.5% on the snow sparkle. The meadow went grey (`DIRT` and `DRY` are the only palette members with g <= r, so over-firing them neutralises green without changing brightness) and the snow gained a white sheen. Every pair was re-solved on the shipped texture's own bytes for the coverage it was tuned against, and the numbers in the shader are therefore *derived* -- re-solve them if the bake changes rather than nudging them.
- **The gradients are packed alongside the value they are the gradient of.** `.gb` hold `0.5 + clamp(dh/du, ±16) / 32`, differenced off the same grid the value came from, so the same fetch that colours a fragment also lights it -- the surface is finally shaded as though it were made of what it looks like, instead of by a second independent noise. The ±16 range is measured, not guessed: equalisation stretches the field's spread, so `|dh/du|` runs p50 2.7, p90 8.3, p99 14.6, max 24.3, and ±16 clips 0.55% of texels where ±8 would clip 11%. A rotated fetch reports its gradient in the *rotated* frame, so the fine sample's `.gb` is turned back through the transpose before it can enter a world-space bump.
- **Tiling is the one thing a texture costs that noise did not**, and it is answered three ways rather than hidden: the two scales off each tile are incommensurate (11.7 and 2.3 first agree after 269 m; 1024 and 137 after 20 km, past the far edge of an 8 km world), the finer of each pair is rotated ~37° -- far from every multiple of 45, and therefore from every way a square lattice can agree with itself -- and the coarsest layer repeats only every kilometre.

What is gained, beyond the frame time: a texture fetch has no precision to lose. The hash noise measured 148 distinct values out of 400 at the origin, 16 at 1 km, and **two** at 6 km -- a comb with a 50 m period, which is what drew the snow flecks in dashed parallel lines. A 256-texel lattice wraps exactly however far out the sample is taken. Mipmaps also do the anti-alias fades for free, which is why the distance fades that remain are *cost* fades rather than shimmer fades.

**The texel size is a look decision, not a range one.** 256 texels over 11.7 m is 4.6 cm a texel, magnified `NearestFilter`, which is deliberately the bench ground from `preview-stage.js` (`grassTexture`, 64 px over 3 m, 4.7 cm texels) at the same texel size -- quantised to six steps and then speckled, so the ground reads as pixelated Perlin rather than as smooth noise. Change `GRIT_METRES` and the ground stops looking like the bench, whatever else it does.

**Every fetch is `textureGrad` and the derivatives are hoisted above all the branches.** Every guard in the shader folds in distance *and* the surface classification, so none of them is quad-uniform -- see the `textureGrad` note under the rock tile below, which is the same argument. One pair of `dFdx`/`dFdy(vWorldPos)` is taken at the top of the colour block and scaled (and rotated) per fetch, since every uv is a plain multiple of world XZ. `check-shaders.mjs` marks `textureGrad( uGritMap` and `textureGrad( uMacroMap` so a regression to implicit LOD is a gate failure rather than a bug report from the headset.

**Keyed to world position, in the fragment shader, deliberately.** Anything baked per-vertex would rescale itself at every quadtree ring and pop as the LOD changed -- the grain would visibly breathe as you walked. Same reason the base classification in `chunk-mesh.js` stays coarse: it is the only part that *can* live on vertices.

**And "keyed to world position" has to include the classifier's INPUTS, not just its output grid.** `shade()` obeyed the rule on paper -- one colour per vertex, no scale-dependent noise -- while breaking it completely, because the steepness it classified on was the mesh normal, a central difference over the chunk's own cell: 1 m at a leaf and 128 m at depth 3. Steepness is what keeps snow off cliffs, and an alpine face standing at 74° over 1 m averages out to 24° over 128 m, so the identical ground came out bare rock up close and solid white from a distance. Over 400 snow-capable sites the white fraction ran **31.5% at the leaf to 55.8% at depth 3, rising monotonically with cell size**; flying away from close terrain repainted the world one chunk-shaped square at a time. The fix is that the classification slope is now measured over a **fixed 1 m stencil** (`CLASS_EPS`) regardless of chunk size, which flattens that to 31.5% → 29.8%. Leaves are exempt and therefore bit-identical, since their cells already *are* the stencil; coarse chunks pay four extra `heightAt` per vertex, once, and then cache. `check-terrain.mjs` guards the drift, because every other drill in the suite watches geometry and bookkeeping and none of them can see a colour.

The general form is worth keeping: **a per-vertex quantity is only LOD-safe if every term feeding it is a function of world position alone.** Mesh normals are not -- they are a function of the mesh.

Three things this pass got wrong, all worth remembering. Vertex colours and plain `THREE.Color` uniforms are **linear working space**, and the palette had been authored as if they were sRGB: linear 0.33 is sRGB 0.60, which under a 2.1-intensity sun came out as pale mint green. Dark gritty ground lives around linear 0.05. The speckle is what makes speed legible -- on untextured ground at 29 m/s you cannot tell you are moving at all. And **no grass constant in this file can be judged on its own**: all four are mix destinations, so what the meadow reads as is the mean of the whole chain over the real fields. Run it (200k fragments, the four samples a near fragment gets) and read `g - max(r, b)` alongside luminance and SD/luminance, which is the contrast the eye actually reads: base `C_GRASS` 0.0400 green at lum 0.0753, the pre-bake look 0.0249 at 0.0652 with SD/lum 0.138, the grey wash 0.0105 at 0.0550, and shipped 0.0239 at 0.0483 with SD/lum 0.294 -- 26% darker than the pre-bake meadow with more than twice its contrast. Eyeballing the constants is how the grey shipped in the first place.

**The macro tile is un-faded, because the fade is what made distance look flat.** Everything past ~95 m was reading as smooth green or smooth grey, and the cause was not a thin palette -- it was that the only thing varying the palette had already faded out. The near grain and the macro layers have opposite requirements: grit *must* die at range or it aliases into shimmer once it is sub-pixel; the macro layers *must not*, and are safe not to because they are never close to pixel-sized from anywhere you can stand. One shared fade cannot satisfy both, which is why they are a separate tile rather than more octaves on the grit one. The coarse macro sample is ungated entirely -- it swings brightness on everything (damped on snow, since blotchy snow reads as dirty snow) and pulls green ground toward a dry ochre or a damp deep green; the fine one is gated on the **haze** rather than on a distance, and stains rock on a decorrelated channel, since mineral banding follows the face rather than the valley.

Gating on transmittance rather than distance is the load-bearing choice there. `scene.fog` is `FogExp2` driven by `clock.js`'s `hazeDensity`, which swings more than 5x over a day -- 0.00113 at noon, 0.00021 at night. "Past 1.5 km it is all hazy blue" is exactly true at the noon value (5.7% of the surface survives) and wrong by a factor of five at the night one (1.5 km still delivers 91%), so a hard-coded distance would erase the snow line's shape on a clear night. Transmittance is the very number the fog chunk is about to multiply the fragment by, so the gate says precisely what is meant: stop paying for detail the fog is about to eat.

### The rock surface wears the boulders' own tile

Everything else in this shader is noise, and noise is isotropic and self-similar -- exactly wrong for rock, which has bedding: bands, fracture lines, a direction. So the rock class samples `rocks/stone.png` (`LAYER.ROCK`, the same tile the boulders wear), triplanar, at **16 m per tile**, with a **fainter 2 m octave** added inside 130 m. The point is not detail for its own sake: a boulder resting against the crag it fell off has to be the same *material* as the crag, and while the prop was a photograph and the face behind it was procedural noise, it never was.

**Divided by the tile's own linear per-channel mean (`ROCK_TILE_MEAN` in `textures.js`), which is what makes it safe to add at all.** The quotient averages `(1,1,1)`, so multiplying by it contributes the photograph's grain and mineral colour while moving the palette not one step darker or warmer -- and every colour in this file was tuned against an untextured surface, so anything that shifted the mean would have invalidated all of them at once. `check-rocks.mjs` measures that multiplier over the real tile and holds it at 1.000 per channel. It is the same inversion the per-instance rock tints use, for the same reason, off the same constant.

**Triplanar is not optional here.** The whole subject is cliffs, and a cliff is exactly where a flat xz projection stretches a 16 m tile into vertical smears. Three fetches; picking the dominant axis instead costs one and draws a seam along every 45-degree edge, which on a mountain is most of the edges.

**`textureGrad`, not `texture`.** Both octaves sit inside a guard that folds in distance *and* the rock/grass classification, so a quad at the foot of a crag has some lanes in and some out. An implicit-LOD fetch in non-uniform flow is undefined per the ES spec, and the way it actually fails is the sharpest mip on a fragment that wanted the blurriest: a line of sparkling pixels down every grass border. The caller takes `dFdx`/`dFdy` of the world position once, outside the branch where it is legal, and both octaves scale the same pair.

The coarse octave's fade is far out past everything else here (220 m to 650 m) and it is a *cost* fade, not a pop fade -- mipping takes the tile to its own mean at distance and the divide turns that into a no-op, so the layer disappears on its own; the smoothstep only stops three fetches being paid for pixels they no longer change. That guard also folds in the rock classification, so grass and snow pay nothing.

**Opt-in, via `createTerrainMaterial({ atlas })`.** Without the atlas no sampler is declared and neither block is compiled, so v1's terrain and the benches are byte-identical to what they were; the two variants carry different `customProgramCacheKey`s so they can never share a program. Only `/v2` passes it, which is why `src/v2/main.js` builds the prop texture array *before* the terrain rather than down with the trees.

### The ground wears a photograph too, and the snow does not

Same argument as the stone, one step finer: `LAYER.TERRAIN_GRASS` is one square metre cut from a photograph by `tools/props/cut-terrain.mjs`, sampled **planar** at 1 m a tile inside a 60 m to 150 m fade, divided by `GRASS_TILE_MEAN` so it averages `(1,1,1)` and contributes grain and blade-to-soil colour swing without moving a palette that was tuned untextured. Planar rather than triplanar because ground is most of the screen and three fetches over most of the screen is not a price a Quest can pay; the xz projection stretches by 1/cos(slope), 15% at 30°, and by the angle where that would show, `chunk-mesh` has already called the fragment rock. At 128 px the tile is 8 mm a texel, so a blade is a texel or two, and mipping is what makes that safe -- as it goes sub-pixel the mips converge to the tile's own mean and the divide turns the layer into a no-op, so `GROUND_FAR` is again a cost fade.

Inside its fade and **on grass only**, the tile turns off the brightness speckle, the dirt/moss mottle and the grass half of the 10 cm micro tint: those are the same job done worse, and running both is two textures at one scale, which reads as mud. What stays is the snow sparkle (a specular stand-in, not a texture), the macro layers (regional, and the tile is not), and the whole normal pass -- the tile carries no relief, and a lit surface needs both.

**There was a snow tile and it did not work, which is the boundary of the whole photograph-beats-noise argument.** That argument is about *structure*: a photograph wins where the surface has real structure at the scale being drawn. Snow has almost none -- a square metre of it is a faint grey shading -- so the tile contributed nearly nothing except the one artefact a tile cannot avoid, and with no high-frequency detail to hide behind, its 1 m repeat became the most visible thing on the snowfield. Grass hides the same period behind blade frequency. Removing it costs nothing and gives back more than it took: the grit speckle, flecks and sparkle had been *suppressed* on snow by the same `auroraProc` mask, so making that mask grass-only hands the snowfield straight back to two incommensurate, mutually rotated scales, which is exactly the property the photograph could not have. One fetch cheaper on every snow fragment inside 150 m.

### Nothing tone maps, so the snow was not bright -- it was gone

`src/v2/main.js` builds the renderer with `toneMapping: none`, so the transfer curve is linear albedo times light, hard-clipped at 1.0, then sRGB. There is no shoulder. `C_SNOW` is 0.88, the sun runs 2.1 and the hemisphere 0.85, so a lit snow face computes roughly `0.88 * (2.1 * 0.8 + 0.85 * 0.9) = 2.15` -- clipped by more than 2x, and clipped at every daylight angle down to a grazing one. That is why the snowfield read as a flat white sheet: the sparkle, the flecks, the grain and the entire relief pass were all being computed correctly and then thrown away by the clamp. Anything over 1.0 here is not bright, it is *gone*, and everything drawn on top of it is gone with it.

The fix is an exposure scale applied **last**, after every mix, as `uSnowAlbedo` 0.40 on snow and `uGrassShade` 0.85 on grass. Last matters: anywhere earlier and each subsequent `mix()` toward an absolute palette colour would drag the surface back up, the additive sparkle especially. Snow on screen at N·L 1.0 / 0.8 / 0.4 / 0.0 went from 2.52 / 2.15 / 1.41 / 0.67 to 1.01 / 0.86 / 0.56 / 0.27 -- a range the eye can read as shape, with the brightest face square to the sun just touching white, which snow should be the thing in this world to reach.

**Why not darken `C_SNOW` in the mesher instead.** That constant does two jobs: it is the snow albedo *and* it is the classification channel this shader reads, since `auroraVertexSnow` thresholds `vColor.b`. Darkening it there would silently stop the shader recognising snow as snow. The mesher's colour is the classification; this stage owns the shading. The consequence to watch is that props carry their own snow (`SNOW_TINT` in `material.js`) and it is clipped exactly the same way, so a snow-capped boulder reads brighter than the ground under it until that one is scaled to match.

### Fine relief is a NORMAL, not a seventh height octave

The ask was bumps and divots on every surface, because the ground was reading as poured and edible up close. That cannot go in the height field, and the reason is a number: the leaf chunk is 16 m over `CHUNK_RES` 16, so the mesh resolves **1.00 m cells**. A sub-metre wavelength is under Nyquist there. It would alias into a pattern that crawls whenever a chunk rebuilds, cost five more `heightAt` evaluations on the collision path (already the frame's most expensive query), and feed `slopeAt` at eps 0.75 with garbage -- manufacturing exactly the sub-metre walk refusals §4 just finished removing.

Perturbing the shading normal buys the look with none of it: geometry-free so nothing rebuilds and nothing can block her, world-keyed so it does not rescale across LOD rings, and inside a fade so it is gone before it can alias.

**The relief now comes free with the colour, which is the second-biggest thing the bake bought.** It used to call `auroraGrad` three times at `normal_fragment_begin` -- nine noise evaluations, thirty-six integer hashes, on top of the twelve the colour block had already run -- off fields that had nothing to do with the ones doing the colouring. Now each grit fetch's `.gb` is the derivative of the `.r` that coloured that fragment, so the colour block accumulates a world-space bump into a file-scope `vec3` and the normal chunk does nothing but `normalize( normal + viewMatrix * auroraBump )`. Two rungs, one per grit sample: the coarse one at the 11.7 m tile's own ladder, the fine one at the 2.3 m tile's, on its own tighter fade (gone by 40 m, where a feature this size is about 3 px) and its own flat surface mask -- full on rock, half on grass and snow alike. The coarse rung gives snow only a fifth, because half-metre relief makes a drift read as gravel; at a centimetre that does not apply, since windblown snow really is pitted at this scale.

**Amplitude was re-derived by measurement, not re-dialled by eye.** `.gb` is `dh/du` in *tile* units, so dividing by the tile's size in metres is what turns it into a real slope -- and it is why two rungs five times apart in size can share one amplitude convention. Running the old GLSL in JS against the new texture's actual bytes: the old two-octave coarse term averaged `|d(noise)/d(metre)|` of 0.3605, so `uRelief = 0.35` bought a mean tangent of 0.126; the baked field averages 5.785 per tile-unit, which over 11.7 m is 0.4945 per metre, so **`uRelief = 0.255`** buys the same 0.126. The old 10 cm octave averaged 4.2576 per metre for a tangent of 0.0766 at `uMicroRelief = 0.018`; the fine fetch averages 2.5153 per metre, so **`uMicroRelief = 0.03`**. The knobs went *down* because the field under them got steeper, not because the look changed.

**This ladder wants to be FLAT, which is the exact opposite of the terrain slope ladder in §3.** Landform coherence needs one scale to dominate -- rungs ~2x apart, or the eye finds no large form. Surface texture needs every scale to read at once, because a real gritty surface differs at all of them simultaneously. The baked fbm inside each tile is five octaves at gain 0.5, which is that flat ladder, and the two tiles put two of those ladders five times apart.

Two implementation details that are load-bearing. The fine rung is added to the **same** bump vector rather than applied as a second `normalize` -- two successive normalizes let the coarse tilt swallow the fine one wherever the coarse tilt is large, which is on rock, precisely where it is meant to be strongest. And it is nested inside the `uRelief > 0.0` guard, so the fill-bound escape hatch still kills the whole normal pass in one uniform.

Note there are three separate operations at the fine scale: the micro tint pair, the snow sparkle, and this. That is not duplication. A real gritty surface differs in albedo *and* in normal at once, and doing only the first is why a flat-shaded hillside with speckle on it still looks like icing.

### Transparency: alpha test, never alpha blend

`material.alphaTest = 0.5`, binary cutout, no partial alpha.

Not merely a preference: alpha blending requires back-to-front sorting, cannot write depth, and **fundamentally cannot be sorted within a batched or instanced draw call**. It is architecturally incompatible with §5. Alpha test writes depth, needs no sorting, batches cleanly.

Two caveats, neither fatal:

- On Adreno tile-based GPUs, `discard` disables early-Z for that draw. Render alpha-tested foliage **after** all opaque geometry so it still benefits from the opaque depth prepass.
- Alpha-test edges alias badly against MSAA. Set `alphaToCoverage = true`; cheap, and it cleans this up.

True alpha blending is reserved for **the aurora**, **fog/mist cards**, **water**, and **snow particles** -- all of which are small in number and sort trivially.
