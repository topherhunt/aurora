## 21. Dead wood

> **Covers:** how `src/props/deadwood.js` builds a standing snag or a fallen log out of one swept surface, the tier ladder and the budget it went over on purpose, the broken rim, the analytic normal and its clamp, and how a piece meets the ground.
> **Read this when:** a log floats, a stump's top goes black, a tier looks like a different object, or you are about to add a knob to `DEADWOOD_DEFAULTS`. The source carries the local "why" at each line; the arguments that took a round of measurement live here.

Dead wood is the forest's LITTER -- the things that hem a path off, break a sightline at knee height, and say a wood has stood long enough for something in it to have died. Snags and logs are ONE generator: a snag is a log stood on end with a broken top. The build makes a tapered tube swept along a crooked spine, caps both ends, hangs branch stubs off it, and then either leaves it standing or lays it over. `kind` decides that last step and nothing else.

### The four decisions

**1. The surface is a pure function of `(t, a)`** -- distance along the spine, angle around it. `radiusAt` is deterministic and stateless and every tier evaluates the same function at fewer rings and sides, so a tier change loses FACETS rather than swapping in a different log. This is rock.js's lesson, and it is a stronger promise here: a tube is far better behaved than a displaced sphere -- an 8-sided log and a 3-sided one have the same silhouette width everywhere, so the sampling gain rock.js has to correct for never arises. It also lets the shape be interrogated without building it, which is what prints a triangle count and a bark fraction before a vertex exists (`deadwoodCost`, `deadwoodRim`).

**2. The cross-section is not a circle and the spine is not a line.** tree.js builds a live trunk as a round cone with one quadratic lean and no relief, and is right to -- a live trunk is hidden by its own canopy. Dead wood has no crown; it is looked AT from two metres, and a lathe-turned cone reads as a fence post. So `ovality` and `lobes` make the section an irregular polygon varying with height, `swell` puts burls and waists along the length, `checks` cuts the radial splits a drying trunk opens, and `kink` bends the spine in two harmonics so it is CROOKED rather than merely leaning. Every angular term has an INTEGER period in `a`, which is what closes the seam exactly at `a = TAU` and keeps decision 1 true at any side count.

**3. Bark comes off, and where it has gone the surface drops.** This is the whole of what makes dead wood read as dead. `barkAt` is a wrapped 2D value-noise field on the cylinder; high means the species bark layer, low means `LAYER.TIMBER_BEAM`, the weathered baulk the buildings wear. The choice is per FACE -- which is the only reason this geometry is non-indexed -- because `texLayer` is an INDEX and interpolating between two of them walks the shader through twenty-four unrelated layers across one triangle. The surface also drops by `barkThick` where bark is missing, so the boundary is a real STEP in silhouette rather than a change of colour; conifer bark is 2-4 cm thick and comes away in sheets, and the step is what the eye reads as a sheet having come away. The `bark` slider is a fraction covered only APPROXIMATELY: value noise spends most of its range near its mean, so 0.5 is about half the surface but 0.2 is rather less than a fifth. `geo.userData` reports the sampled result on a 24x24 grid no tier uses, because the number and its result are not the same thing.

**NO NEW TEXTURE LAYERS.** Bark is the layer the living trees already wear, exposed wood is the beam the buildings already wear, moss and snow are the shared material's uniforms. A snag and a log cost this file and nothing else on disk.

**4. It sits on the ground by being cut off at it.** `sink` drops the piece below `y = 0` and everything under the plane is clamped UP onto it -- rock.js's `sit`, for the same reason: a log pressed into duff is flat where it presses, and a bowed log touching at two points would arch over a visible gap. The cost is a ribbon of degenerate faces along the contact line, which take the shell normal because they have no usable face normal of their own.

**ATTRIBUTES: always** `{ position, normal, uvProj, texLayer }`, non-indexed with an identity index -- the shared prop material's layout (`src/material.js`). An `InstancedMesh` does not validate it the way `BatchedMesh` did, so a geometry short an attribute draws wrong rather than throwing.

### The ladder: two mesh tiers and then a card

**A coarser tier loses SIDES, not RINGS.** Both are cheap to cut and they are not worth the same. Sides buy ROUNDNESS, which at any distance a coarse tier is used at is doing almost nothing -- a 5-sided trunk and an 8-sided one are the same trunk. Rings buy the SPINE, and crookedness is most of what separates dead wood from a fence post; on a fallen log it is most of the silhouette too, because a long log's bend wanders further sideways than the log is thick. Measured on a 3 m log, dropping to one ring took its plan width from **1.03 m to 0.63 m** -- not the same object at any distance. So every mesh tier keeps every ring, and five sides is the floor: the fewest that can still express `jag`, since a jagged rim needs more than one splinter's worth of facets.

**There used to be a three-sided T2** on the argument `ROCK_TIERS` makes for keeping an octahedron -- a lit face, a shaded face, a turning silhouette. Good for a boulder, bad for a stump: a boulder's ladder has to reach past where an impostor is affordable and a stump's does not. `DEADWOOD_LOD_AT` cards a chest-high stump at about 20 m against a cull at 100, so the span T2 would have covered is a photograph instead -- and a photograph of the real LOD0 beats a 3-gon of it at any distance, for one triangle rather than eighteen.

**A STUB IS A SOLID AT T0 AND A CARD AT T1**, and both follow from the same fact: a stub reads at distance out of proportion to its size, because it breaks the silhouette OUTWARD -- a bare trunk with three spikes off it is not the same object as a bare trunk. Near enough to matter it is also the thing the eye lands on, and a cone -- one ring fanning to a point on the axis, `stubSides` triangles -- is a spike rather than a break. T0 draws it as a five-sided prism with a torn end instead: `stubSides` quads up the barrel, then a fan of one triangle per side closing on a point that stands `STUB_TIP_POINT` (0.35 of the length) PAST the rim or the same distance behind it. Even odds either way, rolled once per stub, because a branch that tore leaves a splinter standing proud and one that rotted through leaves a socket, while a rim that is half spike and half hollow reads as neither. The rim itself wanders along the axis by `STUB_TIP_JAG` (0.22), rolled BEFORE the barrel is emitted so the quads reach the wandering ring rather than a flat one with a jag stuck on afterwards, and the fan is emitted FLAT -- a break is facets, and blending the ring's radial normals into it would round off the one feature the extra triangles bought. Three times `stubSides` triangles, 15 a stub. **STUBS THEN SURVIVE TO T1**, as one triangle each: what a stub does not need past 10 m is thickness, so T1 draws each as a single card standing in the plane holding both the stub's axis and world up, which is the plane a drooping stub already leans in and the one whose silhouette IS the stub: rotate the card 90 degrees about the axis and its three vertices project to a line. A card has one normal by construction, and the plane's own normal (horizontal, across the stub) is the only choice that is not a lie about part of it.

**FIFTEEN SIDES AT T0**, and the count is not taste -- it is the lcm of the two angular features the stump carries. `roots` puts 5 buttresses round the base and `jagCount` puts 5 splinters round the top; both are periodic in `a`, and a periodic feature sampled at a side count it does not divide ALIASES. The samples land at a different phase in each lobe, so five identical roots come out as five different bulges wandering round the trunk and five identical splinters come out as a rim savage on one side and conical on the other. Fifteen gives each lobe THREE samples -- one on the fin, two in the gap; one at the splinter tip, two down in the notch -- and three is the floor, because two is Nyquist exactly, where the sampled amplitude depends on where the random phase landed. That is the aliasing back in a different hat. This failure does not throw, does not change the triangle count, and cannot be seen in any number the bench prints. T1 stays at five: both features are 0-10 m features, past which a fin is under a pixel wide.

**T0 WENT OVER THE BUDGET ON PURPOSE.** §5's prop table puts stumps and logs in the `bush` row; the row predates this generator, so it is a target and not a law, but it is the number the world was budgeted against and going over has to be deliberate. Fifteen sides, a fourth ring and solid stubs take a stump from 76 triangles to **210**, so `BUDGET_TRIS` moved with it (to 216, a ceiling over the worse of the two entries) rather than the bench printing a permanent red number nobody reads. What makes it affordable is the BAND and the BANK, not the count: T0 ends at 5 m per metre of size and there are two entries in the whole world, so the pieces paying 210 are the handful within a few strides. A log, which keeps three rings and rolls five stubs, pays 195; past the band everything is on the 5-sided T1 at 54 and 45 or on one triangle of card. A long log holds T0 further out in metres, and that is the ladder working -- it is the same handful of pixels either way.

**The ladder is measured PER METRE of the piece's own size**, and that is the correction it was rewritten for. The thresholds used to be flat distances (T0 to 10 m, T1 to 20) set against the bench's readout on a chest-high stump: a fine number for a stump and nonsense for a log. `LOG_LENGTH` reaches 34.8 m and distance is measured from the instance ORIGIN, so a player standing at a 20 m log's END is 10 m from its origin looking at five-sided geometry from arm's length. What decides whether a triangle is worth drawing is ANGULAR size, so the threshold carries the size term and every piece steps at the same apparent size. Same system as `ROCK_LOD_AT`, deliberately mirrored rather than a second mechanism. `DEADWOOD_LOD_AT = [5, 10]` leaves the median snag where the user set it: a 2.1 m stump (the median of `SNAG_HEIGHT` under its skew) holds T0 to 10.5 m and T1 to 21, the old ladder to within a stride, while the median 5.6 m log meshes to 28 m and cards at 56 and a 20 m one is still a mesh at the cull.

**The metre it is measured in is the longest axis of the box**, one function for a snag and a log, and that is the point rather than a convenience. The two kinds are the same object lying in different directions, so keying on height would card a 30 m log at 20 m and keying on plan width would hold a slim spar's mesh out to nothing. What the ladder asks is how many pixels the piece subtends, and the largest extent a box can present is its longest axis.

**`DEADWOOD_CULL` is 100 m ABSOLUTE**, not relative, because it is a fact about the SCATTER -- the tile grid ends there. 100 m on a 2 m log is 1.6 px of card, under the threshold at which anything is recognisable; but a scatter is not one log, and dead wood thinning out at the same radius the trees do is what stops the layer reading as a ring painted round the player.

### Age is a tint, not a texture

`DEADWOOD_TINT = 0xab9d88`, and nothing else in the world wears it. The obvious alternative was four more atlas layers -- a darkened copy of each bark PNG plus the timber. Dead wood does not have DIFFERENT bark from live wood; it has the same bark greyed and browned by weather, so a per-family multiply says the true thing in one number. It also lands in the right place in the shader: `diffuse` multiplies the atlas sample BEFORE `MOSS_APPLY` / `SNOW_APPLY` mix over the top, so the wood ages while the moss stays green and the snow stays white -- which is what would have to be hand-maintained if the tint were baked into the tiles.

The value is a GREY-BROWN and not an orange one: wood rotting under a canopy goes toward mud and ash, and the red end of the bark tiles is the first thing weather takes out. Its red-green gap is a third of the bark's own, which is what stops a fallen log reading as freshly cut. **And it is a LIGHT multiply**, the correction asked for after seeing the first one in the world: a strong brown multiply reads as a DIFFERENT MATERIAL rather than as weathered wood, and a piece of it lying on the floor is a dark stain instead of settling into the ground. This is the old `0x8f7c5c` pulled **28% of the way to white** -- luminance 0.50 to 0.61, saturation 0.36 to 0.20 -- so it still greys and darkens the bark but leaves the tile's own contrast doing most of the work.

The other half of the correction is not in this file: `GROUND_CUE` in `src/v2/render/deadwood.js` bends each instance toward the terrain hue it is standing on, which can follow the ground from a riverbank to a burn and a constant cannot. The one thing the tint cannot do is age two species differently -- birch going grey is a different journey from pine going red-brown -- and the bank ships one bark layer, so nothing does. The route back is litter's rather than a variant per species: `texLayer` is a plain `attribute float`, so a per-instance `InstancedBufferAttribute` picks the bark without costing a mesh.

### The broken rim is a dealt deck, not a noise field

`jagComb` decides how deep the rim is bitten at each angle. **It is not a noise field, and the two attempts before it were** -- they failed in ways that look opposite and are the same fault.

- **A lattice scaled by the slider** made the slider a CEILING: the field is redrawn per seed, so one stump's peaks saturated the clamp into a shattered top while the next stump's never got near it and came out a smooth cone. Same setting, two different objects.
- **A cosine comb timed by a lattice** fixed that and overshot. Evenly spaced teeth of near-equal depth do not read as a break at all -- they read as a machined crown, because nothing in a rotting trunk has a period.

Both ask one field to carry HOW MUCH and HOW UNEVEN at once. So they are separated. How much is a fixed ladder of notch depths -- `dealt` builds the same multiset on every seed, one deepest notch, one shallowest, the rest evenly spread -- so the total bite barely moves and the deepest notch is always exactly what the slider asked for. How uneven is the DEAL: which tooth gets which rung, how wide each tooth is, where the ring starts. Drawing `n` independent uniforms instead would put a coin flip back into how broken the seed looks, which is the first failure by another route.

Splinter tips sit at cell boundaries and notch floors at centres, with a smoothstep between, so the curve meets its neighbour with zero slope at every boundary: the rim is **C1 all the way round including the seam at TAU**, which is what lets it be sampled at any number of sides. Cell widths are uneven and renormalised to a full turn (`JAG_WIDTH` 0.4) -- a splinter every 72 degrees is a cog. `JAG_SLAB_LO/HI` (0.6 to 2.0) is the exponent on each notch flank, one per SPLINTER rather than per tooth because a splinter is shared by the notches either side and has one width. It is the term that decides whether two neighbouring vertices can both stay up at the rim, which is the readable form of "broken or cut": a spike narrower than the angle between two sides puts exactly one vertex on each and the top is a machined crown however uneven the notches are; a slab is wider and catches two, which on a dealt ladder happens on about half of seeds.

`endT` turns the slider into an amount: `bite` reads `jag` against `JAG_FULL` (3.5), so the setting is a fraction of the deepest bite a rim may take rather than a free multiplier that may or may not reach it -- half the slider really is half the rim on every seed. The `MAX_JAG` (0.4) clamp is belt and braces; the comb cannot exceed 1 by construction. The end RINGS themselves ride `endT`, so the last ring wanders along the spine rather than a cap being stuck onto a clean edge, which is the only construction that leaves no gap and no doubled rim. `deadwoodRim` exists because the rim CANNOT be recovered from the mesh -- a stub tip and a bored funnel both put vertices at heights unrelated to where the wood ends -- and the gate needs the curve to assert that `jag` means the same thing on every seed.

### Normals are analytic, and capped at the horizon of their own face

The ring frame is the fixed local X/Z pair rather than a frame carried along the spine (tree.js's choice, right for the same reason): rings stay in horizontal planes and the spine's lean slides them, so a bend never twists the texture. The bends here are a few percent of the length, so a swept frame would buy nothing and would need a parallel-transport pass to stay stable.

The normal is the real one, `dS/dt x dS/da` with both radius derivatives by central difference. Cheap at build time, and it is what makes a check and a burl catch light instead of being silhouette-only -- pure radial, which is all tree.js computes, lights a flared butt as though it were a cylinder.

**But never past the horizon of its own face.** The analytic normal is the IDEAL surface's, and the ideal surface has features the tessellation cannot hold: a drying check is a groove eight degrees wide whose walls turn the true normal sixty degrees off radial, and at eight sides the vertex landing on a wall sits on a facet spanning forty-five. Where that gap opens past ninety degrees the vertex normal points away from the face it belongs to and Lambert lights the whole triangle as facing away -- one black facet in the middle of a lit trunk. So the lean is capped at `MAX_LEAN` = 0.1736 = cos(80 deg), ten degrees of margin before a vertex normal reaches its own face's horizon, which is enough that interpolation across the face cannot reach it either. Re-aiming keeps the sideways part and rebuilds the along-face part, so the direction the check was leaning in survives. Below the cap nothing moves, which is every face on every variant except the few straddling a deep check: it is a clamp on the TESSELLATION's honesty, not the shape's, and raising the side count stops it biting on its own. **One place it does not reach:** the ground clamp at the end of `buildDeadwood` moves positions after every face is emitted, so a face on the buried belly ribbon gets a face normal these were never measured against. That ribbon is the surface the piece stands ON; it is left alone rather than bought a second pass.

### The end faces

A fan from the ragged rim to a centre pulled `cup` radii INTO the piece. That dish is what a rotten heart looks like and it costs nothing -- the fan has the same triangle count whether the centre is proud, flat, dished, or bored a metre down. Which is why `cup` is allowed past 1: at a dish it is a rotten heart, and at two or three radii the same fan is a HOLLOW STUMP you can see down into for the same sixteen triangles. Depth is clamped at `0.8 L` -- past that the centre comes out of the far end and the fan turns inside out, and on a snag it would take the top face below `y = 0` where the bedding contract (min y exactly 0, gated) stops being true. The floor it leaves is also what stops a hollow reading as a hole punched clean through.

**Always flat shaded, whatever `smooth` says.** §19 records the same rule for the buildings' log ends: an all-smooth log has ends that look like melted wax. End grain meets the barrel at a right angle and has to keep that arris, and it matters more once the fan is a funnel -- a smoothed funnel wall has no rim, and the rim is the whole reason you read an opening rather than a dark smudge. UVs are a planar projection across the axis: end grain, not bark running round a corner.

**The apex is a point ON THE SPINE**, at the depth the bore reaches -- not a point offset from the end along the end's TANGENT, which is what it used to be and is only the same thing on a straight piece. The difference is the whole of `bend` and `kink`, and it is a bug rather than a subtlety: a 2 m log's spine wanders 10-15 cm sideways, so a funnel bored 60 cm along the tangent ends that far OFF the pith and its wall -- a straight line from rim to apex -- cuts out through the barrel on the side it drifted toward. What you see is a triangle of the inside of the log poking through the outside, lit from the wrong side, moving as you walk round. Aiming at the centre of the ring section at the bored depth cannot do that, because every point of the wall is then a chord of a section the barrel also passes through. `t` is arc length to within a percent, so `depth / L` is the parameter step, and it is measured from the MEAN RIM rather than from `t = 0` or `1` -- otherwise `jag` and `cup` fight and a rim eaten 40% down gets a bore measured from where the piece would have ended had it not broken.

**The layer is the same question the barrel asks**, and this one is a judgement rather than a fact. It used to be the wood layer at both ends unconditionally, on the reasoning that a break face is by definition where the bark is not -- sound for a snapped trunk, and silent about a top that rotted away, where the crumbling rim carries on out of the bark on the sides. Running `faceLayer` keeps both readings on one dial: at `bark` 1 the top is still skinned, at 0 it is end grain everywhere, in between it is patched like the sides. **A hollow's wall is the one place this is arguably wrong** -- punky rotted heartwood is what you see down a hollow stump, never bark -- but the wall is drawn by the same fan as the rim and cannot take a different layer without splitting the fan in two. Left as is: a hollow deep enough to look down is dark enough that its wall reads as depth rather than as a material.

### The winding bug, and what it generalises to

Both caps were once inside out. The top of every snag was a backface -- reported as "the top face is just black". **Not by being culled and not by being backlit**, and the route matters because it says which faces are at risk. The prop material is `DoubleSide`, so nothing is culled; it also applies `normal *= faceDirection` a second time over three's own, and twice is the identity, so the lit normal is the AUTHORED normal on both sides and lighting there is winding-independent. Neither saved this cap, because on a FLAT-emitted face the authored normal is NOT independent of the winding -- `emitTri` derives it from the vertex order, so reversing the order reversed the normal. The stored normal measured `(0, -1, 0)` on the top of every snag: dotNL 0 from the sun, the hemisphere's ground colour underneath, and back-of-normal darkening on top. Black.

**The lesson generalises the opposite way round from how it looks:** on this material a winding error is invisible ANYWHERE the normals are authored independently -- a smooth barrel, a radial limb cone, a leaf card given the canopy shell's normal -- and visible ONLY where a face's normal comes from its own winding. Flat shading is what couples them.

**It survived the winding gate because that gate was a tautology.** The gate compares each face's normal against its own stored vertex normals, and a cap is emitted flat, which makes the stored normal a COPY of the face normal: the assertion compared the value to itself on precisely the faces that were wrong. `check-deadwood.mjs` now also asks a snag's caps which way they point in the WORLD, which the geometry cannot answer with a tautology. A funnel needs no second case: as `cup` drives the centre through the rim plane the face normal swings continuously from straight up to up-and-inward, the correct outward side of a bored hollow, and its Y component never reaches zero however deep the bore goes.

### How a piece meets the ground

**The trunk is what rests on the ground, and the stubs are allowed through it.** The `minY` used to run over every vertex, which made a branch stub decide where the whole piece sat: one stub drooping under the barrel is the lowest point on the mesh, so the piece was lifted until the STUB touched and the trunk hung above it. On a log with four to six stubs round a 3 m barrel that is not an edge case but the usual outcome -- the log rests on two spikes with daylight along its belly, the one artefact that says "placed" rather than "fell". So the min and the clamp are taken over the TRUNK only (`trunkFloats` watermarks where the stub loop starts, and `userData.trunkVertices` publishes it so a check can see the same line). A stub that ends under `y = 0` passes into the soil, which is what a branch under a fallen trunk does. It costs the triangles of the buried part, and that is the right trade: trimming stubs against the plane would make a stub's length depend on how the piece was bedded, and a tier's stub card would no longer match its prism.

**A log lies IN the ground, not on a tangent to it.** `sink` alone drops the piece until its single lowest vertex is a fraction of a radius under the plane, which is the whole answer on a snag because a snag's underside is one flat butt. On a log it is not: the underside is a 2-8 m line following a spine that bends and kinks, so sinking the lowest point buries that point and leaves everything either side in the air. **A 3 m log at the default bend and kink floats its ends by about 10 cm**, and a gap under a fallen log reads as a bug from every angle. So a log also sinks by the full vertical wander of its own spine, which is exactly what puts every point of the underside at or below the plane and lets the clamp flatten it into one continuous belly ribbon. Measured on the SPINE rather than the vertices, because the vertices carry the barrel's radius, which is not wander and would bury the log by its own thickness. A butt diameter caps it, and the cap is what keeps `pitch` usable: a deliberately propped log has metres of wander and must not be swallowed whole.

**The origin is the middle of the footprint, not the butt.** Everything builds from the butt outward because that is where the spine starts -- fine for a snag, where the butt IS the middle, and useless for a log: a 3 m log built that way hangs three metres off its own origin, so a scatter that places it at a point puts it anywhere but there, its bounding sphere is twice the radius it needs, and -- the one that shows -- the billboard card, centred on the origin by construction, stands a metre and a half from the mesh it replaces. That is a prop that jumps sideways at the LOD switch. Centred on the SPINE's own range rather than the vertex bounding box, because the box is a property of the tier (an 8-gon and a 5-gon catch different lobes) and a centre that moved between tiers would put the nudge back in a smaller form. The spine is the same curve at every tier, which is also why it is walked at a fixed 32 steps rather than the ring count: every tier must bed at the same depth and stand in the same place or the LOD switch nudges the log.

**`measured.buttDiameter` is the widest the base actually gets**, not the nominal `butt`, and the difference is the flare -- which since `roots` is a crown of buttresses reaching nearly twice the trunk's radius rather than a collar a third wider. It matters because of who reads it: `render/deadwood.js` seats a piece by this number, sampling the ground at that radius round the butt and sinking by `tan * radius` on a slope, so a value that understates the footprint leaves the downhill fin hanging in the air. Sampled rather than solved, because `radiusAt` carries ovality, lobes, checks and the bark step as well as the flare and there is no closed form for the maximum of the sum; four times the finest tier's side count is 48 calls once per geometry.

### Stubs, and where they are allowed to be

A stub is a broken-off base, so it is a short straight prism with a torn end and no curve -- not `growLimb` and not a branch. The one thing worth copying from tree.js is where it STARTS: 0.6 of the local radius INSIDE the drawn surface, so the stub is seated in the wood rather than balanced on its skin. A stub starting on the surface hovers beside the trunk the moment anything bends. They are spread by the golden angle plus a jitter so two never stack up the same side however few there are, and `stubRise` is measured from horizontal and defaults low: a stub angled up like a live branch reads as a tree that is still trying.

`stubEnd` is a **band cap and not the fix**. `jag` and `stubs` are computed independently and the rim is the one that moves, so a stub at 0.95 of the length on a piece whose top was eaten back to 0.6 grows out of thin air. But the rim is a function of ANGLE, so no single number is under all of it -- a stub at 0.64 under a notch that bit to 0.60 still floats. The fix is in the stub loop, which pulls each stub under the rim at its OWN azimuth using the same `endT` the end rings ride, asking the wood where it stops instead of guessing with a constant. The margin is the stub's own radius in `t` units, because clearing the rim by a hair still leaves the upper half of the prism outside the trunk. `stubEnd` stays because it also controls where stubs LOOK right, which is the lower two thirds of a snag -- not because it is load-bearing.

### Seeded streams, and the rule about adding a knob

Four independent streams (`shape`, `bark`, `jag`, `stub`), tree.js's lesson: a shared stream means adding any new cosmetic knob shifts every draw downstream, so tuning `stubs` silently reshuffles the bark. The same rule bites ACROSS TIERS inside the stub stream, which is one stream walked over the stubs in order: the torn end takes a sub-stream seeded from a draw made at every tier and spent only at T0, because a tier that consumed its own tip rolls straight from `stub` would move every stub after this one and the tiers would stop being the same solid at different sampling rates. The gate measures exactly that, tier against tier, on all three axes. Within the shape stream the same rule applies to ORDER -- `rootPhase` is drawn LAST on purpose, because everything above it feeds a knob that already existed and nothing is downstream of it to shift. It has to exist at all or every stump in a species puts its roots at the same six compass points.

`cylLattice` is value noise on an (angle, height) lattice that WRAPS in angle and clamps in height. The wrap is the whole trick and is not optional here the way it is on the bench ground: without it every bark patch is cut in half by a seam running the length of the log, at the one angle the UV seam is also at, and the two together read as a stripe painted down the trunk. The lattice is sized so patches are roughly square on the surface, floored at 3 cells around -- a two-cell wrap has only one independent value and gives a barber pole.

The kink harmonics are anchored at `t = 0` by subtracting their own value there, so the butt stays where the placement put it however hard the slider is pulled; otherwise `kink` also translates the whole piece sideways and reads as a broken dial.

`uRepeat` is derived rather than dialled -- how many tiles fit round the butt at the density `texMetres` sets going up, ROUNDED TO AN INTEGER so the seam at `a = TAU` lands on a tile boundary. tree.js derives its own the same way. `texMetres` is therefore the one texture dial and means the same thing in both directions. The seam vertex is DUPLICATED (`k` runs one past `sides`): the same point in space at `u = uRepeat` rather than 0, because sharing it would run the whole tile backwards across the last face.

### The variants: axes, and what rolls per seed

`LOG_DEFAULTS` is a SECOND DEFAULTS BLOCK rather than a variant, because it is not a shape choice -- it is the handful of dials whose right value genuinely depends on the attitude. `DEADWOOD_DEFAULTS` is authored for a standing stump (what the bench opens on), and these are wrong for a log:

- **`length`** -- a stump is what is left of a trunk; a log IS one. A short piece is what a forest floor is littered with; a whole fallen tree is a landmark and gets placed by hand.
- **`flare` 0.1, `roots` 0** -- a root flare belongs to the end still in the ground. A log broken off above the roots has almost none, and what little it has is a SWELL, not a crown: buttresses are what a trunk makes where it dives into soil, and six fins on the end of a piece lying on its side read as a cog.
- **`cup0` = `cup1` = 2** -- both ends open and bored right out. A stump has one end in the soil and one rotted top; a log has two breaks, both at eye level to somebody walking past, and a break in a rotten trunk is a hole rather than a dish. This is the cheapest detail on the piece.
- **`stubEnd` 0.95** -- stubs run the WHOLE length. A standing snag's top is a rotted rim and stubs stop short of it; a fallen log's far end is a snap through live wood, and branches were growing right up to it.
- **`bend` / `kinkFreq`** -- a log is the only one of the two long enough for a lean to read as a lean rather than as a base that will not sit flat, so it keeps the crooked spine the stump gives up. Without it a 3 m log is a dowel.
- **`rings` 3** -- those metres are straight trunk. The stump spends its fourth ring on the run between the root crown and the broken rim.

**`jag0`, `jag1` and `stubs` ROLL per seed rather than being authored** (`LOG_ROLLS`), and this is not the same choice as an axis made twice. `jag0` used to BE an axis, taking 0.35 or 0.15 with the variant name carrying which. An axis is for a difference you want to be able to PLACE -- the scatter picks a variant, so an axis is a knob the world can point at a spot. How chewed one particular log's ends are is not that: it is the natural spread within one kind of object, and the whole reason two logs side by side do not look stamped. Rolling it is free where an axis is three draw calls -- but at `DEADWOOD_SEEDS` 1 there is one roll rather than a spread, so every log in the world is currently the same mesh and its variety is the matrix's. The seed count is the dial that buys the spread back, and it is priced in draw calls like everything else here. Both ends roll the same range: once the value is per-seed there is nothing to spend the far end on keeping quiet for, and a log with one savage end and one demure one is a shape, not a rule. Ranges 1-2 read against `JAG_FULL` are a quarter to a half of the rim's appetite -- ragged breaks rather than the eaten-out crater a 3.5 stump top is. Stubs 4-6 rather than the old flat 2: the underside branches broke off in the fall, but a whole trunk carried more than two to begin with, and a bare pole reads as sawn timber.

The roll happens in `deadwoodParams` rather than inside the builder, and that is the right place: what comes out of that function IS the shape, so the bench's sliders show the rolled values, the seed spinner walks them, and anybody wanting a particular log can copy the numbers out. A roll hidden in the builder would leave the bench lying about what it drew. The stream is hashed from the NAME as well as the seed, so one seed does not hand the log and the stump the same three numbers.

**One log at 4 m and one stump at 3 m, and there is no length axis.** The scatter rescales both -- `LOG_LENGTH` runs to 34.8 m -- so what the authored length sets is not the size the player sees but the PROPORTION the shape was drawn at: a 4 m log at its authored butt diameter is a slimmer thing than a 2.5 m one would be, and that survives the rescale. One value per attitude because `PropArena` draws one `InstancedMesh` per (tier, variant), so a second length is three draw calls, where a second SIZE is free -- it is a uniform scale on the instance matrix. Three metres and not one for the stump because the top of a stump is where all its detail is, and `jag1` eats up to `MAX_JAG` of the LENGTH getting there: a 1 m stump gives up 40 cm of itself to its own broken rim and has 60 cm left to be a trunk in.

**The bank is TWO ENTRIES, and everything else is the seed and the matrix**, which is the opposite of `rock-bank.js` and the difference is worth stating. A rock's twenty-five entries each answer a question about a PLACE -- a riverbed rock and a summit rock are different objects with different silhouettes, and no product of axes would have produced either. Dead wood has no such spread: every piece is the same swept surface, and what distinguishes two of them in a forest is which way up it is and how badly the broken end is chewed. The first is the entry; the second rolls per seed; size, yaw and lean are the instance matrix's and cost nothing. It lives in the source rather than the bench for the usual reason: a preset table only the previewer could see would let the shape signed off and the shape placed drift apart.
