## 11. Water

> **Covers:** lakes, rivers, and the water shader.
> **Read this when:** touching water. Note that the world currently ships fully drained (§2).

From the Phase A hydrology (§2):

- **Lakes:** one flat plane per body, at the spill elevation from priority-flood. Built and shipping -- greedy-merged horizontal runs, one mesh per 64-cell tile so frustum culling has something to cull, and the mask dilated one cell so the polygon edge is buried and the shoreline comes from full-resolution terrain crossing the plane rather than from the sim grid's staircase.
- **Rivers and streams:** ~~ribbon mesh extruded along the splined flow paths~~ **built, measured, removed.** The ribbons read as paint rather than water, and the reason is not fixable in the renderer: Phase A routes flow over the *carved* surface, and the chunk mesher renders the *raw* one. On the rendered surface **47.4% of river segments run uphill** (0.26% on the carved surface), evenly across every size band, and steepest-descent tracing on the rendered surface pits out after a median of 39 m. A ribbon can be made to sit flush -- fixed 10 m resampling took buried vertices from 14.9% to 36 of 36,527, worst 4.5 m -- but a flush ribbon climbing a valley wall is still wrong, and it is wrong about the one thing rivers are for. **Rivers become possible when the generator produces a surface that drains, i.e. fluvial erosion at generation time (§2), not before.** The three measurements are recorded in `src/water.js`'s header so this is not re-attempted blind.

#### PINNED: `2026-0827-badass-cubemap-v1`

This is the reflection approach as of 2026-08-27, recorded so it can be returned to rather than reconstructed from memory. Everything below in this section describes it; this block is the parameter set and the shape, in one place.

**Shape.** Reflected sky is analytic (`skyRadiance` along R, shared with the dome). The aurora is added from a 64 px additive cube (`sky-probe.js`, `{size: 64, everyNFrames: 2, gain: 1.0}`), stars and sky deliberately excluded from it. The land -- trees, bank, buildings, rock -- comes from a 128 px RGBA half-float cube of the ordinary scene (`world-probe.js`), whose **alpha is coverage**. `worldSilhouette()` takes `max(horizonMap, captureAlpha)` and `mix(uSilTint, captureRGB, coverage)`, and is called by both the top face and the underside so the two sides of one surface cannot disagree. Fresnel mixes body against reflection; no quantisation anywhere; no screen-space anything.

**Waves.** Five octaves of gradient noise with analytic derivatives, the three fine ones inside a distance branch and domain-warped by the largest. `WATER.flow 0.5`, `warp 0.6`, `detailFrom/To 45/380`. Layers as `wavelength / slope / speed / detail`:

| λ (m) | slope | speed | detail |
|---|---|---|---|
| 52 | 0.055 | 6.75 | no |
| 21 | 0.05 | 21 | no |
| 7.5 | 0.052 | 12 | yes |
| 2.6 | 0.042 | 7 | yes |
| 0.65 | 0.03 | 3.5 | yes |

Rotations 13/41/97/152/206, headings 17/74/131/168/214, offsets `[148.2,402.7] [317.4,-88.1] [-604.9,251.3] [72.6,933.8] [-441.5,-170.2]`.

**Probe.** `WORLD_PROBE = {size: 128, height: 0.2, duck: 1.2, everyNFrames: 30, moveRefresh: 12, fadeSeconds: 1.0, mix: 1.0}`. Faces `[0,1,2,4,5]`, -Y skipped. Two cubes ping-ponged with an eased cross-fade; re-anchor refused while one is in flight.

**Reflection tint.** `reflTint 0xc2d4ee`, `reflDim 0.8`. **Underside.** `skyGain 0.6`, `distort 2.5`, `window 0.78/0.3`, `veil 0.18`, `underFog 0.45`, `tir 1.4`. **Surface.** Fully opaque -- `WATER.clarity` did not exist yet; setting it to `0` restores that.

**Diverged since, deliberately, and these three are the whole list.** `WATER.clarity 0.1` makes the top face see-through where she looks steeply down into it (below); `UNDERWATER.underFog` is gone, the underside now taking the bed's full murk fade rather than a 0.45 share of it; and `UNDERWATER.tirLit 0.4` lights the mirror outside Snell's window instead of painting it flat murk. Nothing about the reflection itself -- the cubes, the alpha-as-coverage, the silhouette, the Fresnel mix -- has moved.

Not yet tagged in git -- to pin it there too, `git tag 2026-0827-badass-cubemap-v1` on the commit that carries this.

Shading -- **built, and it is a mirror rather than a blue plane.** Only about a quarter its own colour at normal incidence; the rest is sky, and some of that quarter is now the bed (`clarity`, below). What was planned above and what shipped differ in two places, both because something already in the world turned out to answer the question better:

- **The sky reflection is not a cubemap.** `sky.js` already computes the sky analytically from a world-space direction, so the water calls that same function along the reflected ray. One implementation, no second render pass, no 8-bit round trip -- and the reflection is automatically correct at every time of day including sunset, because it *is* the sky. The shading body moved to `sky-glsl.js` so the dome and the lake share it and cannot drift apart; `check-water-shader.mjs` fails if a second copy is ever started.
- **The waves are not normal maps, and they are not sines either.** The first pass was six summed directional wave trains with their headings carefully spread across a half turn so nothing would beat into a moire. It tiled visibly anyway, and the reason is not fixable by choosing better headings: *a sum of periodic functions is periodic.* Six sines repeat on the lattice whose cell is the least common multiple of their wavelengths, and the eye finds that lattice in about two seconds -- the lake reads as wallpaper sliding past. What replaced it is five layers of **gradient noise with analytic derivatives**, each with its own lattice rotation, its own sampling offset, its own drift heading and its own drift speed, and the three fine layers **domain-warped by the largest one** so they are dragged around by the swell instead of merely lying on top of it. Still gradient only -- the plane is never displaced, and the analytic derivative avoids picking a finite-difference step size that would be wrong at some distance no matter what it was. The three fine layers sit inside a distance branch, which is coherent because neighbouring fragments are at neighbouring distances, so the far half of a lake pays for two noise evaluations rather than five.

  **The fifth and smallest layer is 65 cm, and its slope is the smallest of the five rather than equal to them.** It was added because the surface, correct at every scale down to 2.6 m, had nothing at the scale a person standing on the bank actually looks at -- the catspaw texture that reads as "water" before any wave does. 65 cm is exactly a quarter of 2.6 m, keeping the octave ratios even. The slope is not: `slope` in the table is a **tilt**, not an amplitude, so reusing 2.6 m's number at a quarter the wavelength would make a 65 cm feature as steep-sided as a 52 m swell, and the lake would come out a rasp. It is a `detail` layer, so it lives inside the distance branch with the other two and is domain-warped by the swell.
- **The wave speeds are unphysical on purpose, except the swell's, which is now the slow one.** 21 m/s for a 21 m feature is roughly a river surface, not an ocean swell. The first pass was physical -- 1.35 m/s, a 27-second period -- and the note back was "the large octave ripples appear to not move at all!?", which was exactly right: at that speed the biggest layer crosses its own wavelength once every half minute. 20x turned out to be past the mark in the other direction, so `WATER.flow` -- the one knob that scales all five layers together without disturbing their ratios -- now sits at 0.5, landing the surface at 10x physical. `check-water-shader.mjs` asserts the laziest layer stays under 4 s, so halving it again would fail rather than quietly drift back to a lake that does not appear to move.

  **The largest octave is exempt from that bound and runs at a quarter the others' pace** (6.75, against the 27 it shared with them). Large waves genuinely do move slowly relative to their own length, and at 27 the 52 m swell crossed itself in 3.9 s, which read as the whole surface sliding rather than as a swell passing under it. At 6.75 it takes 15 s. This is safe to exempt precisely because it is one layer: the four underneath it are untouched, so the surface cannot go back to reading as frozen without them going slack too, and the gate is written that way -- every layer *but* the largest is held to the original 4 s, and the largest is only required to be moving at all. Slowing a second one trips it.

  It also slows the **domain warp**, which is the largest layer's own gradient and is what drags the three fine layers around. So the fine detail keeps its own drift and loses the swell-speed component it was inheriting on top, which is most of what "the large waves move too fast" actually was. The knock-on: the 21 m layer is now the fastest-crossing of the five at 2.0 s, and is the next thing that will read as too quick if this one did not go far enough.
- **The tilt is measured, not derived.** A summed-sine surface could be checked from its table alone, because cos() peaks at 1 and the worst tilt is the sum of the slopes. Gradient noise hands you no such bound, so `check-water-shader.mjs` ports the same noise to JS and samples 20k points over 8 km and 60 s: median 4.5 deg, p99 10.8 deg, peak 14.6 deg. The port is not bit-identical to the shader and does not need to be -- the hash returns a *unit* vector from a uniformly distributed angle, so the distribution of gradients is the same on either side of the language boundary. The same file checks that the field does not echo itself at any multiple of a layer wavelength, which is the failure the sines had.
- **Fresnel term:** yes, and it is what makes the far end of a lake read differently from the near end.
- **You can see into it, and `WATER.clarity` is a whole rendering decision on a dimmer switch.** At `0` the material is `transparent: false`, the alpha branch does not run, and the pinned look is reproduced exactly -- which is the point of deriving the material flag from the knob rather than writing a literal, and `check-water-shader.mjs` asserts that derivation. At `0.1`, where it sits, the surface transmits a tenth of itself where the view is both close and steep. The ramp is `1 - uClarity.x * down * near`, where `near` is the same distance term the ripples fade on -- so "close enough to resolve the waves" and "close enough to see the bottom" are one number and cannot disagree -- and `down` is a **geometric** gate, `smoothstep(sin(clarityAngle), 1, -V.y)`, shut above `WATER.clarityAngle 45` degrees of depression and fully open straight down.

  **The angle gate is geometric because Fresnel was the wrong shape.** The first version rode `1 - f`, which reads as "the more mirror-like the water, the less you see through it" and is exactly right physically. It is also nearly flat where it matters: `pow(1 - cos, 5)` is still 0.88 at twenty degrees below the horizontal, so the bed came through most of the way to the horizon -- forty-five metres of clear water seen from a shoreline, which is a swimming pool and not a lake. The replacement asks the question that was actually being asked, which is about the *viewing angle* and not about reflectance. It reads `-V.y`, which for a unit view ray **is** the sine of the depression angle, so no trigonometry happens in the shader; the degrees-to-sine conversion lives on the CPU in the uniform, once. And it reads the flat view ray rather than the wave normal on purpose: taken off the normal, the window would open and shut with every ripple.

  **The divide is the non-obvious half.** Blending returns `src * a + dst * (1 - a)`, so an alpha of 0.6 dims the reflection by 40% as well as letting the bed through -- the lake goes *dark* exactly where it goes *clear*. Pre-dividing the colour by alpha cancels that, leaving the full reflection plus `(1 - a)` of what was behind. Most of it, not all: the divide is in linear and the blend happens after the colour-space conversion, so the hardware mixes sRGB-encoded numbers. That is true of every transparent material on the web and this one is not the exception.

  **It transmits more than Fresnel says it should, and that is `mirrorDown`'s fault rather than this knob's.** The physically exact ramp is `1 - mirror`, which caps at `1 - 0.72 = 0.28` and is barely visible. Real water reflects about 2% at normal incidence; `mirrorDown 0.72` is an artistic number standing in for a body colour that is now partly the actual bed. Shaped by Fresnel, not bounded by it, so the two knobs stay independent.

  **Two things it deliberately is not.** It is not refraction -- bending the background sample means reading the frame as a texture, which is the one thing that does not survive WebXR, whereas *blending* is XR-safe because the blend unit runs per eye inside the layer. And it is not depth-aware: there is no bed depth here, so a puddle and a twenty-metre pool are equally clear. `depthWrite` stays on despite `transparent`, because this is a sheet and not a cloud -- one water fragment per pixel -- and everything drawn after it must stay occluded by the lake the way it was before the knob existed. Seen from below the underside forces alpha 1 regardless: there is no bed behind the ceiling, only `scene.background`, and blending against that punches a hole to the clear colour.
- **Sun and moon highlight:** a *broadened* lobe, not the sky's hard disc. A 1.1 deg disc sampled through a rippling normal lands somewhere different every pixel -- that is static, not glitter, and it crawls whenever the head moves. So the disc is switched off in the reflection path and the highlight is drawn with a lobe that widens with distance, in step with the wave normal relaxing toward flat over the same range. The two fades are the same idea applied twice and neither works without the other.

  **Sizing that lobe by eye is a trap, because the knob is an exponent and the eye is measuring an angle.** For `pow(cos t, n)` the half-maximum half-angle is `sqrt(2 ln2 / n)`, so apparent size goes as **1 / sqrt(n)** -- halving the distant moon's blob costs a *factor of four* in the exponent, not a factor of two. The far end went 190 to 760 on exactly that arithmetic, 4.89 deg down to 2.45 deg, with the near end left alone at 2600 because up close it was already right. Both ends are named knobs now (`sharpFar`, `sharpNear`) and the check asserts the 4x-is-2x relation directly, so the comment explaining it cannot drift away from the numbers. The cost is real and worth stating: the broad far lobe is half of the distance anti-aliasing, so a tighter one buys realism back at the price of a little more sparkle crawl on distant water when the head moves.
- **Mountains occlude the reflection**, using the horizon map §8 already bakes for terrain shadows: it stores, for every point and 16 compass directions, how high the ground rises, so asking it along the reflected ray says whether that ray reaches sky or hits a ridge. Two texture reads the terrain was already paying for. Deliberately lo-fi -- 16 azimuths and a point sample, so it is a soft rounded silhouette, not a ridgeline. Where it blocks, the surface takes the mountain's **own deep blue** -- hue only. Dimming a grey dawn gives a grey mountain, and what reads as land-against-sky is a shift in hue as much as one in brightness.

  **The brightness is computed, not tuned, and matching it to the terrain is the whole trick.** The first attempt scaled the sky's horizon luminance by a floor and a gain, and it was consistently too bright for a reason no choice of gain fixes: the horizon is the *brightest* part of the sky, and a shadowed hillside is not a scaled copy of it. So the CPU now evaluates three's own Lambert indirect path once a frame for one specific reference fragment -- the darkest albedo in the terrain palette, on a vertical face, in the far field, with the directional light fully blocked -- and the silhouette is painted at exactly that luminance. Every term is the dark end of its range, so the result is a floor rather than a mean, which is the correct side to be wrong on when the failure being fixed is *too bright*. The darkest albedo is exported from `terrain-material.js` and derived from the palette array itself, so a colour edit cannot leave a stale number behind in the water. At full dark `farAmbient` reaches 0 and the silhouette goes honestly black, because the distant terrain it stands in for does too.

  **A correct silhouette was not enough, and why not is the more useful lesson.** With the silhouette landing exactly on the darkest terrain, a blocked patch of water was still visibly *lighter* than black rock at noon, and across a whole day-night cycle its brightness moved by only **1.29x** when it should move by an order of magnitude. The silhouette was not the problem. The shader composites `mix(body, refl, mirror)`, and `WATER.tint` -- the colour of the light that gets *into* the water, bounces around inside it and comes back out -- was a **constant**. The `(1 - mirror)` share of a constant reaches the eye at every viewing angle, which makes it a floor under the entire lake that no amount of occlusion can get under; at full dark, where the silhouette is legitimately zero, that floor was **100%** of what remained. Doubling the silhouette's darkness, the obvious next move, would have made the measured symptom *worse*: noon falls from 9.64e-3 to 8.13e-3 while midnight stays at 7.47e-3, flattening the very curve that was the complaint.

  Two changes, and it is the pair that works. First, `WATER.tint` is now the colour as authored **under a midday sky**, scaled every frame by the ambient sky luminance normalised to the clock's own noon keyframe -- so daylight water is pixel-identical to what was tuned (the ratio is exactly 1 there) and the body goes **13.7x** darker at its darkest. Sky only, no ground bounce, because this is a body lit from above through a horizontal surface; no night lift and no `farAmbient`, because lift is airglow on a Lambert surface and this is not one, and `farAmbient` is a *near-field envelope* that would make the water at your feet a different colour from the water ten metres out. Second, `blocked` now takes the **body** to the silhouette colour as well as the reflection -- physically for the same reason, since the light that entered the water came from the same piece of sky the mountain is standing in front of. With both sides of the Fresnel mix at the silhouette colour, a fully blocked patch composites to exactly that colour **at any viewing angle**, which is what makes the terrain match hold at grazing incidence and not just looking straight down.

  Replicating another library's shading maths on the CPU is a copy, and copies drift silently -- a three minor release moves a chunk, or `lighting.js` changes what it multiplies, and the only symptom is a silhouette that is quietly the wrong brightness. So `check-water-shader.mjs` pins each line to its source: string matches against `lights_pars_begin`, `common` and `lights_lambert_pars_fragment` for three's half, against `lighting.js` for ours, and then an end-to-end sweep of all 201 sun elevations asserting the uniform equals the reference formula at every one. It deliberately does **not** assert monotonic darkening: the measured curve rises about 0.4% of noon during twilight because `skyGlowAmt` climbs faster than `hemiIntensity` falls, the terrain does the same thing there, and following it is the point. The body tint gets the same treatment -- a 261-elevation sweep against its own reference formula, plus an assertion that it really does end up under a fifth of its daylight value, which is the assertion that would have caught the constant-floor bug on the day it was written.
- **The reflection comes back darker and bluer than what it reflects.** One multiply, and it is the term that stops the lake reading as a hole cut through to a second sky. Physically it is the light the surface transmits instead of bouncing, and water swallows red first. Applied to the sky only and not to the glitter, which has its own gains -- otherwise two knobs end up fighting over one number.
- **Sun and moon glitter is a THRESHOLD, not a falloff.** `pow(d, sharp)` is the statistical answer -- the average over every facet inside one pixel -- and it is right for water too far away to resolve a wavelet and wrong for water at your feet, where a facet either points at the light or it does not. Real glitter is crisp specks of blown-out white in dark water with nothing in between. So the lobe is thresholded, and how hard rides on the same `near` term everything else does: hard up close, relaxing back to the smooth lobe at range. Thresholding *distant* water instead would make every pixel a coin flip as the head moves, which is the specular aliasing the distance fades exist to prevent. The gains are all above 1, so the core clips to white and only each speck's rim keeps the tint of the body that lit it.
- **Far water ends in the air the land beside it ends in.** A lake fades with distance at `scene.fog.density` like everything else, toward the terrain's own aerial ramp at its own distance: `uAirNear`/`uAirFar` shared by reference from `WorldLighting` and mixed at `AIR_FALL` exactly as `AERIAL_GLSL` mixes them (§8, §10). The unfogged part of a far lake is a mirror of sky; the fogged part is in-scattered air, and the air over a lake is the air over the ridge next to it, so at full extinction the two are one colour and a hazed valley reads as one air rather than a valley with pale patches in it. Under rain `haze`, `fog` and the horizon are one grey and the lake goes with them; after dark the fog is pulled below the sky to hide the far terrain the moon cannot light, and it takes a lake with it exactly as far out as it takes the land, so a far lake at night is still the dark sky it mirrors. The ramp's ends are raw sRGB components, mixed after `colorspace_fragment` on every other material; this shader fogs in linear *before* it, so the ramp is mixed in sRGB as the land mixes it and linearised once (`sRGBTransferEOTF`). Mixing linearised ends instead leaves a lake a shade off the land at every distance.
- **No screen-space reflections.** Far too expensive, and at night nobody will miss them.
- **The aurora IS reflected, via a probe -- and the sky and the stars deliberately are not.** The aurora is a mesh with a dozen noise evaluations per vertex, not a function of direction, so the analytic path structurally cannot see it. `sky-probe.js` captures **it and nothing else**, and water *adds* the result -- which is exactly how it is composited into the real sky, so the two paths agree by construction rather than by tuning. Capturing the sky as well would be a downgrade: a low-res capture of a smooth night gradient bands, which is the whole reason `sky.js` was never a cubemap. Splitting it this way is what makes the probe cheap enough to want: it carries only soft additive light, so 64 px a face is plenty, there is no gradient in it to band, and switching it off tomorrow would cost the lake its aurora and nothing else.

  **Not the stars, and the reason is a unit mismatch rather than a cost.** Stars are `Points`, and `gl_PointSize` counts *framebuffer* pixels, not angle. The 1.1-4.5 px speck that is correct on a 1500 px screen covers the same 1.1-4.5 px of a 64 px cube face -- 1.5 to 6.3 **degrees**, against the ~0.05 degrees a real star subtends. All 2400 of them at 30-100x size is a lake reflecting gravel, which is exactly what it looked like. No size fixes it either: a star has to live under a pixel (see §13), and the probe's bilinear filter would smear a sub-pixel point back into nothing. Stars in the water would need a different representation entirely, and are not worth one.

  **The unit of work is one FACE, not one capture.** The cost is not in the fragments -- 64x64 is 4k pixels -- it is all in the aurora's vertex shader, 11 slots x 181 samples x 10 rows = 19,910 vertices of fold, swoop, flare and hem noise. Six faces in one frame is the spike worth avoiding, so each update renders a single face and five updates come round. That fixes the per-update cost at one aurora vertex pass regardless of cadence, and it means the lever for making this cheaper is *less work per update*, not *fewer updates* -- the better trade, because a slower cadence shows as the reflection lagging the aurora while a thinner slice of work shows as nothing at all. Five faces, not six: water folds the reflected ray into the upper hemisphere, so -Y is never sampled. Half-float, because the aurora's dim end sits below one 8-bit step. Parallax is a non-issue -- the aurora is 5.5-17.6 km out, so a capture reused for 100 m of walking is about a degree off.

  **The layer trap, which is the reason this note is this long.** The obvious implementation moves the mesh to a private layer and points the probe camera at it. In WebXR that is a bug in a very good disguise: three does `cameraXR.layers.mask = camera.layers.mask | 0b110` and then `cameraL &= 0b011`, `cameraR &= 0b101`. Layers 1 and 2 belong to the eyes and the mask is *three bits wide*, so an object on any layer above 2 is drawn by **neither eye** -- perfect on the desktop canvas, invisible in the headset. So nothing is moved: the aurora stays on layer 0 and is additionally *enabled* on layer 3. Object layers are a mask and a camera draws when the masks intersect, so the eyes still see it via layer 0 and the probe camera -- an ordinary camera, outside the XR path -- sees it and nothing else via layer 3.

  Two more failures that are silent and photogenic, both now checked headlessly against a stub renderer: `CubeCamera` leaves all six cameras **unrotated** until `updateCoordinateSystem()` runs, which only its own `update()` calls -- miss it and every face captures the same slice of sky, uniformly wrong and never obviously broken; and leaving `scene.background` set fills all five faces with flat fog grey, turning the lake into a mirror of the fog.
- **The trees, the bank and the buildings come from a second probe -- a 128 px cube of the ordinary world, `world-probe.js`, deliberately allowed to be seconds out of date.** The horizon map answers "is there a mountain this way" and can never answer "is there a spruce this way", so the surface reflected a slate-blue skyline over water whose far shore was three metres off. This captures layer 0 itself, and differs from the sky probe in three ways that each matter: it has a **depth buffer** (a hillside in front of a tree has to win, where an additive aurora could not occlude itself), it is **RGBA cleared to transparent black** so that **alpha is the payload** -- 1 where geometry covered the pixel, 0 where the ray reached sky, and a card's own alpha at the soft edge of a leaf -- and it is taken **just above the water rather than at her head**, because a grazing reflection is made of nothing but the horizon and a capture two metres up puts the horizon in the wrong place.

  **"Just above the water" needs a floor under it, and not having one was the grey blobs.** `WaterSurfaces.levelAt` is a yes/no test against the water POLYGON, not a nearest-water query -- and the lake mask is dilated a cell so its polygon edge can be buried under the bank. So standing at a lake's edge it answers with the lake's still level while the ground under her feet is metres higher, and anchoring at `lakeLevel + 0.2` put the capture camera *inside the bank*, with a 0.3 m near plane, where every face it took was filled by whatever card, strip or rock happened to be within arm's reach. That is what "giant grey blobs instead of the mountainsides" was. A river never showed it because a river's level sits close to the ground beside it.

  Her eye is the one point guaranteed to be above the ground, so the anchor is never allowed further below it than `duck`, 1.2 m against a 1.65 m eye -- about knee height, well clear of the ground and still 1.2 m nearer the water than her head. Where she is genuinely *in* the water her eye is at or below the surface, so the floor falls under `surfaceY + height` and the surface offset wins, which is the case it was written for. The floor applies on **both** branches, including the one where there is no water under her at all: leaving that one at her eye would keep the two answers 1.2 m apart and a step across the polygon edge would still move the capture point -- which was the other half of "it jumps around a little bit randomly based on some proxy of your position".

  **One function answers "what is the land along this ray" for both sides of the surface.** `worldSilhouette()` is called by the top face with the reflected ray and by the underside with the refracted one, so the two faces of one mesh cannot disagree about where the bank is. It takes the **max** of the horizon map's coverage and the capture's alpha, never a blend: 128 px resolves a distant ridge in a handful of texels, and its zero must not be able to punch a hole of sky through a mountain the horizon map is certain about. Where the capture has no colour the result falls back to the flat `uSilTint`, so the horizon map is the floor and the capture only ever adds. This is also the whole answer to "can we see the world through the surface from underneath": a screen-space refraction would need `EffectComposer`, which renders to its own framebuffer rather than the XR layer and would therefore exist on the desktop canvas and be **missing in the headset** -- the same trap §5 records. A cube has no such problem.

  **It refreshes on movement, not on time, and the cadence is set by what it is for.** One face every 30 frames while she stands still, five faces to a cube, so a full turnover is about two seconds -- what changes on that scale is the light, not the land. Move more than 12 m from the anchor (measured in 3D, because descending through ten metres of water changes the overhead reflection as surely as walking does) and it re-anchors and refills all five faces on consecutive frames. A cube is correct from exactly one point and this one's subjects are metres away rather than kilometres, so the parallax error is real and unfixable short of a raytracer; what makes it acceptable is that the sampler on the other end is a wave normal wobbling several degrees a second, seen across a lake or through a metre of murk. It reads as "the bank is over there and it is green", which was the entire ask. `WORLD_PROBE.mix` fades it back toward the flat silhouette if that ever stops being true.

  **A re-anchor is cross-faded, not cut, and there are two cubes so that it can be.** The slow one-face-per-30-frames refresh is invisible -- same anchor, only the light has moved -- but a re-anchor changes all five faces at once and the whole reflection jumps to a different vantage point in a single frame. So the new capture is filled into whichever cube is *not* currently being displayed, and only once all five faces are in does `uWorldFade` begin walking toward it, over a second, eased at both ends. Both textures are bound once and never reassigned; what moves per frame is one float. The mix takes alpha along with colour, so a treeline appears by growing solid rather than by sliding in as an edge. Both ends are branched out of the shader (`uWorldFade <= 0.0`, `>= 1.0`) -- the condition is a uniform, so the branch is coherent, and the several seconds between fades do not pay for a second cube fetch per water pixel to mix by exactly zero.

  **A re-anchor is refused while a fill or a fade is still running**, and that policy is the part that matters. There is only one spare cube, so a second burst would have to overwrite the one mid-fade; and at flying speed 12 m comes round faster than a second, so honouring every crossing would end every handover in the snap this exists to remove. Deferring rate-limits the whole thing to one smooth handover per ~1.1 s and lets the cube go staler in between -- which is the way round this was asked for. The first cube at load snaps rather than fading, because the target it would fade from has never been drawn into and is transparent black everywhere.

  **What still shifts between captures, and it is not the probe.** Trees are re-tiered every frame by distance to the *main* camera (`Trees.update(camX, camY, camZ)`), and the scatter is a camera-following disc -- so trees past the draw radius do not exist at all. A cube taken while flying high therefore contains a different set of trees, at different tiers, than one taken on the ground, and the colour balance moves with it. That is the LOD story, it is real, and fixing it would mean re-tiering the forest a second time around the probe each burst, which costs more than the artefact does.

  **Four things are hidden for the duration of every capture, and each is a bug if you forget it:** the water itself (or the reflection contains a 128 px reflection), the sky dome and `scene.background` (either would fill every face with alpha 1 and turn "is there land here" into "yes, always"), and the aurora and stars (which the sky probe already carries, and which would otherwise be added to the water twice). They are hidden by **`.visible`, not by a layer** -- the layer trap above forbids the tidier version -- and each is restored to the visibility it actually had rather than to `true`.


### Underwater

Her head goes under a v2 lake or river and the world changes -- **built**, and the striking thing about it is how little of it is shader work. `UNDERWATER` in `water.js` is the whole knob table; `applySubmersion` in `v2/main.js` is the whole of the plumbing, one function called last in `applySky`. Checked by `check-underwater.mjs`, and the water shader now compiles under `check-shaders.mjs` alongside everything else.

- **The tint, the darkening and the 20 m ceiling are the aerial-perspective model told a different story, not a post-process.** §8's chunk already replaces `#include <fog_fragment>` on every material in the scene and already fades each surface toward `uAirNear`/`uAirFar` at a rate of `scene.fog.density`. Underwater is that same machinery given one colour at *both* ends of the ramp instead of two, and an extinction two orders of magnitude faster. Terrain, trees, rocks, grass, litter, mushrooms and buildings all follow without knowing anything happened, at no cost at all, and -- because it is per surface rather than screen-space -- identically in both eyes.

  **A full-screen pass was the alternative and it is the wrong trade twice over.** There is no composer in this project (§5), and three's post-processing renders to its own framebuffer rather than the XR layer, so the effect would exist on the desktop canvas and be missing in the headset, which is the one place this world is looked at (§17). The blur that would have justified a composer was dropped for the same reason; what actually reads as "you cannot see well down here" is the contrast loss, and the fog already delivers that.

- **Visibility is authored as a DISTANCE, and the conversion to a density is where the bug would live.** `UNDERWATER.visibility` is 20 m; `murkDensity()` turns it into the extinction coefficient the aerial chunk and the water's own fog term both read. The chunk keeps `exp(-(d*density)^2)` of a surface's own colour, so "visible" needs a threshold -- 2% left at exactly `visibility`, which is comfortably past where anything reads as anything in a medium that never truly cuts off. The square being *inside* the exponent makes the relation `1/d` rather than `1/d^2`, so halving the visibility exactly doubles the density; the gate asserts that directly, because a rewrite into a lerp between two authored densities would break it and would look like a fog knob wanting a nudge rather than like a bug.

- **The murk exists in two colour spaces and they must not be interchanged.** `murkLinear` goes to the water shader, whose fog mix happens in linear *before* `colorspace_fragment`; `murkAir` is the same colour as raw sRGB components and goes to `uAirNear`/`uAirFar`, whose mix happens *after* it -- the same reason three uploads `fogColor` unconverted. Both are derived from one hex, so an edit cannot leave one behind, and both wrong ways round look exactly like a colour someone chose. The gate pins that they are the same colour and are not the same numbers.

- **The surface seen from below is one material and one branch, not a second mesh, and the cull is what picks the side.** `setSubmerged` sets `material.side` along with the flag: `FrontSide` while she is dry, because the underside of water is never in view unless her head is in it, and `BackSide` while she is under, so the tops of every surface are culled and what survives is the underside overhead. That is also what keeps a lake's plane from showing through the river she is swimming in at its mouth. The shader reads only `uSubmerged`, never `gl_FrontFacing`, which the earlier `DoubleSide` material made unreliable: three draws a transparent `DoubleSide` material in two passes per mesh with the winding flipped on one, so the facing read true on the underside and the underside branch never ran. Toggling the side is a GL state change with no `needsUpdate` and no program behind it, so a dive compiles nothing, and every water mesh costs one draw instead of two.

- **The underside is the world through the surface, seen through a soft Snell's window.** Looking up you get `skyRadiance` along a direction the wave normal has bent, plus the probe's aurora bent with it, dimmed by `skyGain` for the light that bounces off the surface instead of coming through. Looking along the horizontal you get none of it. The physical answer there is total internal reflection, and with no screen-space reflections to mirror *with*, what the fade lands on is a sheen built out of the murk -- which is what the water in between her and it looks like anyway, so the cheap answer and the right one nearly coincide. *Nearly*, and the gap is the whole of the `tir`/`tirLit` paragraph below: murk alone is the fog colour, and a ceiling painted the fog colour is invisible through the fog.

  **The shape of that fade is the whole bullet.** The first pass used a squared cosine, on the argument that a hard 48.6-degree cone would crawl inside a rippling surface. It crawls, but a squared cosine is not a soft cone, it is a *shut* one: down to 0.44 where the window should be wide open, and in the noise across the entire band she actually looks through from a metre or two under. The surface read as a flat blue plane. It is a `smoothstep` between `windowShut` and `windowOpen` now, straddling `cos 48.6 = 0.661` and 85 per cent open there, and `check-underwater.mjs` asserts both the straddle and that opening -- because "technically nonzero at the critical angle" is exactly what the broken version was.

  **`veil` is the one knob here not chasing physics.** A window that reaches pure sky in the middle is a hole punched through to the sky, and a hole does not read as a surface. It keeps `veil` of murk over even the clearest part, in the murk's own colour rather than a tint of its own, so that the window and the water around it stay one medium.

  **The mirror outside the window has to ripple *and* be lit, and `tir`/`tirLit` are those two things.** Total internal reflection is physically correct and visually a dead end here: what it reflects is uniform murk, so being right about it produces one flat colour across the majority of the surface. `tir` manufactures the ripple, from how far each facet leans into the look (`N.xz . V.xz`, scaled hard because wave tilts run ~0.1), stepped, and used to *lighten*; it falls off as the view tips up, which is exactly where the window takes over. That was not enough on its own, because the colour it was lightening was `uMurk` itself -- the ceiling came out the exact colour of the water hanging in front of it and vanished into its own medium, which is what "it uses that grayscale murky misty look" means. `tirLit` is the fix and it is one mix: the mirrored water is *lit from above by the same sky the window is showing*, so the sheen is `mix(murk, sky * skyGain, tirLit)` -- silver at noon, near-black at three in the morning, tracking the scene for free because the sky value is already in hand. Not the sky itself, which reads as the window never closing. The window's `veil` is made of that same sheen rather than raw murk, or the boundary between veil and mirror draws itself as a visible edge.

  **The underside takes the bed's full murk fade, from the same `waterFogAmt()` the top face uses, and the two half-measures either side of it were both wrong.** A partial share (`underFog 0.45`) left the rim part-crisp and greyed the rest; no fade at all left the rim where the surface meets the bank crisp against a bed already lost in the murk, which reads as a plane intersecting terrain rather than a lake. Light off the far ceiling crosses the same twenty metres of water as light off the far bank, so the two are lost together and the seam between them cannot be seen. The fade is exp2 in distance, so the metre or two straight overhead is untouched -- under 4% murk at 2 m against 20 m visibility -- and what recedes is the far end. What keeps the full fade from reading as fog with ripples is `tirLit`, not a reduced fade: while the sheen was flat murk, any fade at all made the ceiling vanish into its own medium, which is the picture the partial share was judged on. `check-underwater.mjs` counts the mentions of `waterFogAmt()` in the assembled shader and expects exactly three -- the definition and one call per face -- and asserts the underside's is the full mix toward `uMurk`, so neither a fourth fade nor a reduced one can appear without this being revisited.

  **The distortion is free, and it is well over 1 on purpose.** It is `N - up`, the wave normal's own tilt, and the caller computed `N` one line earlier for the top face -- same `WATER.chop`, same four noise layers, so the underside ripples in step with the surface she just came through. But this water's tilt runs about 0.1, so the first pass's `distort: 0.4` bent the view by two degrees, which is a pane of glass. Physics agrees with the bigger number: water to air, Snell's law *amplifies* angles by ~1.33, and the amplification runs away toward the critical angle, which is where most of this surface is seen from.

  **The ridges show through it, for nothing.** `wlBlocked` -- the same 16-azimuth horizon map the top face silhouettes its reflections against -- answers "is there a mountain along this direction" just as well from below, and the terrain is already paying for the texture. So the skyline is on both sides of one surface by construction, in the mountains' own `uSilTint`, wobbling with the waves because it is looked up through the same bent direction as the sky, and the probe is scaled by `1 - blocked` so the aurora does not shine through a mountain. **What it does not include is the trees**, which are not in a terrain horizon map and never will be. That is what the world probe below adds, and the horizon map stays underneath it as the floor rather than being replaced.

- **The fade to the air is switched to the murk under the surface.** Everything above about a distant lake ending in the air is an argument about the air in between, and it holds only while that air is air. Under the surface the far target is the murk like everything else's: a lake across twenty metres of lake water is not brighter than the water in front of it, it is gone. Left fading to the air, a top face across the way reads as a lit hole in the murk, which says "sheet of polygons" louder than a painted surface does.

- **The sky dome, the stars and the aurora are hidden while she is under, and the asymmetry in restoring them is the part worth remembering.** The dome carries `fog: false` -- correctly, since it *is* the distance -- which makes it the one thing in the scene the murk cannot reach, so leaving it drawn rings every lake with bright sky exactly where the surface mesh runs out. `scene.background` takes the murk so the pixels no geometry covers come out right. The dome has to be switched back on explicitly because nothing else sets it; the other two must not be, because their own `update()` decides their visibility from the hour every frame and forcing them true would hang stars in a midday sky. The water shader is unaffected either way -- it gets the sky from uniforms and the aurora from the probe's cubemap, neither of which cares whether a mesh is drawn.

- **`applySubmersion` runs LAST in `applySky`, and being the later writer is what keeps it to one function.** Everything above it writes the world as seen through air, straight from the palette; it overwrites the half-dozen values that are not true underwater. There is no mode for anything upstream to know about, no flag that can be left set, and surfacing is simply the frame where it stops overwriting. `WorldLighting.setAir` is documented the same way: a later writer over two uniforms `update()` sets every frame, not a state of its own.

- **The world capture is taken in air, even while she is under, and it needs a hook to be.** The cube is anchored 20 cm *above* the surface -- it is a picture of the world from the air -- but it runs inside a frame whose atmosphere `applySubmersion` has already sunk: fog at the 20 m ceiling, both ends of the aerial ramp on one colour, both lights dimmed and pulled toward blue-grey. Captured through that, the cube comes back with its distance flattened into haze and its near props grey, *and that cube is what shades the underside of the surface*. The murk then arrives on the sky she is looking up at through the water, having already been applied to the water she is looking through -- twice, in series. That is what "nearby props look passable if a bit gray-monochrome-tinted, but the distant props fade into a murky light-blue-gray haze" was, and it is a *plumbing* bug rather than a colour one: no amount of tuning the underside can fix a capture that was taken in fog.

  `WorldProbe.update` takes an optional `{enter, leave}` pair and calls it around the one face it draws. `enter` is `liftAir(state)`, which restates the same absolute writes `applySky` makes from the same clock state -- so it is a real *inverse* and not an undo; `leave` is `sinkAir()`, the murk tail lifted out of `applySubmersion` so the frame and the hook share one implementation. Sinking is deliberately **not** idempotent (the light lines multiply, because they are dimming this hour's own answer rather than landing on a fixed number), which is exactly why the bracketing is what the gate drives rather than reads: a `leave` that is skipped leaves the palette's air standing for the main render, and one frame in thirty of daylight underwater is a flicker nobody can trace. A dry frame passes `null` and pays nothing.

  **The air swap has to be the OUTER of the two swaps**, and getting that backwards crashed on the first frame she ducked under. The probe's own swap sets `scene.background` to null for the length of the capture -- alpha zero is its payload -- and `sinkAir` writes the murk *into* `scene.background`. Nested inside, `leave()` runs against a null. So the order is: air on, probe state swapped, render, probe state back, air off, and neither half can see the other's null. The gate drives it with a stub scene that has a real background colour and a fake hook that touches it exactly the way the real one does; a stub left with the default `null` background cannot see this class of bug at all.

- **The current is a difference, not an increment, and that is the whole of it.** While she is under, `currentDrift(t, strength)` says where the water has carried her *relative to her own path*: two incommensurate sines along a fixed heading (23 s and 9.5 s, split 0.7/0.3) plus a smaller perpendicular term on a third period, giving about 1.45 m of travel side to side over a couple of minutes without repeating. One sine would be a metronome, and a metronome is the version that reads as a bug -- you feel the turn coming. The function is pure, so the caller keeps the offset it last added and adds only the change: her displacement from her own path is always exactly the function's answer and never an integral of it, which is what stops a 70 cm push walking her across the map in a minute. It lands *before* `player.update`, so the world clamp and the ground test see where she actually is -- 70 cm added afterwards is enough to put her inside a bank with nothing left in the frame to push her out -- and it eases in and out over 2.5 s, because the push arriving in one frame is a shove and a shove in a headset is how you make someone ill. It is lateral only: a vertical component would move her eye across the surface and strobe the entire effect at the waterline. `levelAt` cannot tell a lake from a river, so it applies to both.

- **Caustics -- the moving net of focused light on the bed -- are terrain-only, and that is a decision rather than a limit.** The net needs a world position per pixel and the terrain is the only thing that has one going spare: the fragment patch carries `vWorldPos` already, and the vertex-patched `plain` rung -- the one the headset ships -- is handed a `vWlBed` that exists in a single build of a single material. Rocks and props take the murk and the darkening and simply do not catch the net; lighting them properly would mean that varying on every material in the world to serve the few that are ever under a lake.

  **It is not the water's own wave field, and could not cheaply be.** Caustics are the *second* derivative of the surface -- where the refracted rays converge, not where they point -- and `water.js`'s field is built to return a normal, the first. What is used instead is two ridged noise layers, `1 - |2n - 1|`, at different scales drifting different ways and multiplied: the ridge turns smooth noise into thin bright filaments along its half-level, and two of them cross into a net that moves rather than slides. The honest claim is that it moves like caustics, not that it is caustics.

  **The drift rates are in cells per second, not metres, and they are eight times what shipped first.** `p` arrives already divided by `causticScale`, so at a 1.6 m cell the first layer crosses about 115 cm of bed a second. Twice was not enough and neither was four times: the eye reads a caustic net as *moving water* only when the threads reorganise at roughly the rate ripples cross, and anything slower reads as a projected texture on a floor. The two layers keep their own rates and their own headings -- what makes it look alive is them shearing past each other, and scaling both by one number keeps the shear intact. There is a ceiling and it is the noise field's own cell rather than taste: past about one cell a second the net stops reorganising and starts *sliding*, because a thread can be followed across the screen. The first layer is at 0.72, so there is one more doubling left in this before it goes.

  It is added at the top of the fog slot, so in **output space** and **before** the aerial mix: after tone mapping the threads survive intact instead of being rolled off exactly where they are brightest, and being ahead of the mix means a bed twenty metres off loses its caustics to the murk along with everything else rather than glowing through it. `causticFade` is a different quantity from `visibility` and both are needed -- one is light lost between the surface and the bed, the other light lost between the bed and the eye, and a deep pool has a dark floor however close she swims to it. The whole thing sits behind a uniform branch, so on the fragment rungs it costs nothing while she is dry.

  **On `plain` it is a second program rather than a uniform branch, and that is what lets the shipping rung have it at all.** That rung is patched per vertex and has no world position in the fragment stage to branch on; switching the net on inside one program would interpolate a `vec3` across every hillside in the world to light the beds. So `lighting.patch` takes a `caustics` flag, `v2/main.js` compiles the plain rung twice, and the ground is swapped to the wet build at the waterline -- `CAUSTIC_ARM_M`, one stride of headroom above the local surface, deliberately wider than "submerged" so the first compile lands while she is wading rather than on the frame her head goes under. Dry, the ground is drawn with a program byte-for-byte identical to the one that shipped without caustics, which is the point and is what `check-shaders.mjs` bans `vWlBed` and `wlCaustic` from the dry build to keep true.

  **It dims to `causticNight` after dark rather than switching off**, because a moon over open water really does cast this, and it rides a ramp on the **sun's** elevation from -6 to +4 degrees rather than on `isNight` -- a threshold would pop at dusk. The sun's elevation and not `state.lightIntensity`: that number swaps bodies at exactly -6 degrees, so reading it would make the net *brighten* as the moon took over.

- **The submersion test asks `levelAt` a different question from the one the prop scatter asks.** The scatter wants the authored footprint -- treating a river's widened overhang as wet would strip a band of props off both banks -- and the eye wants the polygon, because being under the water you can see is what being underwater means. One flag, the same 3x3 block and the same scan, widening each sample by paths.js's `drawnHalfWidth`, the `hw + min(RIVER_WIDEN, hw * RIVER_WIDEN_FRAC)` that `ribbonVertices` is handed, so the query and the geometry cannot drift. The level it answers is each river's NEAREST segment's -- the quad the ribbon draws there -- and never the highest of every segment whose reach covers the point: a reach runs a half-width past the segment's own ends, so on a grade the highest is up to `grade x hw` upstream, a metre of water over her head while she stands on the bank. Cost was never the issue: what made `levelAt` careful is one call per scatter candidate, and this is one call a frame. It remains an upper bound through a hairpin, where the ribbon also narrows by its circumradius -- a few centimetres, on the inside of a turn, which is where a river is least likely to be over her head.

- **`eye-water` on the panel is the readout for all of this**, signed metres from the eye to the water standing over it. The surfaces are drawn flat at exactly the `y` the query returns -- there is no vertex displacement anywhere in the water shader -- so `mode: under` sitting beside a positive number is the whole class of waterline bug on one screen, in the one place it can be read: inside a headset, where a console is no use.

- **Two things it is not.** There is no swimming -- she reaches the lake bed by terrain-following into a carved basin, with no buoyancy, no stroke and no breath. And the waterline transition is a hard cut: `uSubmerged` is a float rather than a bool so that softening it later is one line here and none in the shader, but the half-in-half-out shot needs a clip plane and two passes and is not attempted.
