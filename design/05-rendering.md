## 5. Rendering architecture

> **Covers:** the quadtree LOD scheme, `LOD.triDeg`, the triangle budget table, `BatchedMesh` vs `InstancedMesh` (and why the Quest 2 answers that question differently), and the prop LOD ladder.
> **Read this when:** anything renders more or fewer triangles than it did. This is where the budget lives.

### The load-bearing decisions

1. `THREE.BatchedMesh` **for props,** `InstancedMesh` **for the far card bands.** `InstancedMesh` draws N copies of one geometry. `BatchedMesh` uses `WEBGL_multi_draw` to draw many *different* geometries in one call, provided they share a material. The entire prop library -- every tree variant, rock, building, and LOD tier -- collapses into a handful of draw calls. Per-object frustum culling is built in. LOD switching is `setGeometryIdAt(instanceId, geometryId)`: one call, no rebuild, no change in draw call count. **But** `BatchedMesh` **charges for that culling every frame, per instance, on the CPU** -- see "the crossover" below, which is why the far foliage bands do not use it.
2. **One material for all props**, backed by one `sampler2DArray`. See §9.
3. **Alpha test, never alpha blend, for anything batched.** See §7.

4. **Terrain goes through** `BatchedMesh` **too** -- decided at build step 2, and not what the original draft assumed. See below.

Water, sky, and weather each get their own material and their own draw calls. That is expected and budgeted.

### Terrain is batched, for the same reason props are

The first draft of this section said terrain "gets its own material and its own draw calls. That is expected and budgeted," on the assumption that a quadtree produces a handful of chunks. It does not. At the shipped step-2 parameters the selector returned **502 leaves**, which as one `THREE.Mesh` per chunk is 502 draw calls against the 60 the §0 measurements allow -- before a single tree exists.

So terrain uses one `BatchedMesh` with a pre-allocated pool of fixed-size geometry slots, and streaming a chunk in is `setGeometryAt()` on a recycled slot rather than an add/remove from the scene graph. **The whole 16 km world is one draw call.**

This is legal only because every chunk has identical topology -- same `CHUNK_RES`, therefore the same vertex and index count -- so a freed slot always fits whatever arrives next. **If chunk resolution ever varies by LOD level, the slot pool has to become size-classed.** That is the constraint to remember before "optimising" coarse rings to a lower resolution.

Verified on `three@0.180`: `setGeometryAt` reuses a slot in place and throws only if the incoming geometry exceeds the reserved counts; it re-clones `boundingSphere` from the source on every call, so per-instance frustum culling stays correct across reuse; and the batch index widens to `Uint32` automatically once the pooled vertex count passes 65535.

### Hole-free LOD swaps: three separate things have to hold

The reported artifact was a square of terrain blinking out to the horizon colour for a few frames while flying *away* from close detail, then reappearing low-poly. Not wrong terrain -- **no** terrain, with the sky showing through. Guarded now by `scripts/probe-hole.mjs`, whose worker stub delays replies by six frames; the existing checks never saw any of this because their stubs answer inside the requesting frame, so no chunk is ever actually absent.

1. **Every node needs a loaded ancestor.** Depths 0-2 (21 chunks, 256 m cells) are pinned forever so an unloaded node can always draw *something*. They were never being built: `_seedBaseLayer` pushed its requests onto `terrain.queue`, and `_select` replaces that queue wholesale from the desired set on the first `update()`, before `_pump` has ever run. Measured on a settled camera: depths 0 and 1 were **0/1 and 0/4 resident**, depth 2 was 4/16, and the only reason those four existed is that they were far enough away to be desired in their own right. `_loadedAncestor` was therefore returning null ~2100 times over a 2 km back-away, and a node with no stand-in draws nothing. The base layer now has its own queue that selection cannot discard.

2. **Coarsening has to look DOWN the tree, not up.** A coarse node is not in the desired set while you are standing on top of it, so it is never requested and the LRU drops it; receding puts it back in the set cold. Its children are resident and cover exactly the same ground -- so `_select` tries a complete cover of loaded descendants *before* an ancestor. All-or-nothing: a partial cover is the same hole in a more interesting shape. With this disabled, backing away falls through to the base layer and draws 256 m cells sitting 69 m below the ridge; with it, the coarsest cell drawn within 250 m stays at 4 m and the worst error is 6 m. This is the mechanism the request asked for -- the low-poly tile phases in *before* the high-poly one is released -- and it costs no slots, because a cover is made of chunks that were already resident and already being drawn.

3. **Eviction has to be counted in slots, not cache entries.** The cap used to apply to `cache.size`, which counts queued entries holding no slot at all -- a different number by a hundred or more while streaming. A frame that requested 150 chunks would push the cache over its cap and evict ground that was being drawn to make room for nodes that own nothing yet. That is a hole *produced by the eviction policy*, and it appeared the moment the base layer started working. The target is now ready (slot-holding) entries, set to `SLOT_COUNT` minus the in-flight cap so every reply that lands between two `_evict` calls is guaranteed a free slot -- which is what makes the "slot pool exhausted" throw unreachable rather than merely unlikely.

Stand-in retention is bounded (`maxReady − desired − 21`) and spent nearest-node-first, and `_evict` will reclaim stand-ins as a last resort rather than throw. Measured peak: 39 chunks over the desired count, 704 of 768 slots, zero missing sample points across a 85 m/s back-away, a 60 m/s climb, a full spin, and a 765 m/s stress run that cannot stream fast enough and correctly degrades to the base layer instead of to sky.

### What actually binds, per resource

Triangles are the only budget this document tracked for its first six months, and they are not the one that binds for a dense foliage carpet. Measured on desktop (`tmp/` benchmarks, 2026-08-24), these are the four separate ceilings and what each one is actually made of:

| Resource | Cost, measured | What it scales with |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Triangles** | ~350k/frame on Quest 2 | tris/prop x visible props |
| **Per-instance CPU** | **37 ns / visible instance / frame** in `BatchedMesh.onBeforeRender` with `sortObjects` on (24% of it is the sort; 31 ns with sorting off) | instance count, *regardless of triangle count* |
| **Placement CPU** | `TerrainHeight.heightAndSlopeAt` is **4,912 ns**. The hash that yields position, tint, scale, yaw and variant is **34 ns** -- 145x cheaper | terrain field queries, not randomness |
| **Fill rate** | a 2 ferns/m² carpet with cards to 510 m is **1.73x one eye buffer** of alpha-tested quads, and **82% of that is the nearest 12 m** | near-field density, *not* draw distance |

Four consequences, all of which contradict something an earlier draft of this section assumed:

- **A billboard is not cheap because it has 2 triangles; it is expensive because it is an instance.** At 76,000 individual cards -- what 2 ferns/m² with density halving per distance doubling produces out to 510 m -- `BatchedMesh` spends 2.8 ms/frame on desktop, ~11 ms on Quest 2, on nothing but culling and sorting. That is the whole frame. The triangles for the same scene are 208k, which Quest 2 can nearly afford.
- **Draw distance is almost free; near density is not.** The 25-510 m card band costs 0.15x the eye buffer in fill. The nearest 12 m costs 1.42x. Pushing cards from 60 m out to 510 m is a rounding error on fill rate; doubling density at your feet is not. So **push the draw distance out and tune the near density down**, which is the opposite of the instinct.
- **Procedural randomness is free and terrain queries are not.** See §6 -- placement must sample a cached raster, never the height field.
- **Below ~10 triangles per instance, the mesh class matters more than the mesh.** Hence the crossover below.

### The `BatchedMesh` / `InstancedMesh` crossover

Verified against `three@0.180` sources and benchmarked:

`BatchedMesh.onBeforeRender` loops over **every** allocated instance every frame -- reading its matrix, transforming its bounding sphere, frustum-testing it, and pushing survivors into a list that is then sorted. That is what buys per-instance culling and per-instance geometry selection, and it costs 37 ns each.

`InstancedMesh` has **no** `onBeforeRender` **at all**. It is one draw call, culled as a single object against its own bounding sphere. Per-instance per-frame CPU is exactly zero. What it gives up is per-instance geometry choice (one geometry per mesh) and per-instance culling.

So the rule is not "batched is better", it is:

> **Many geometries, few instances ->** `BatchedMesh`**. Few geometries, many instances ->** `InstancedMesh`**, tiled.**

The near bands are hundreds of instances across dozens of variants: `BatchedMesh`, as built. The far card bands are tens of thousands of instances of *one* geometry: `InstancedMesh`, one per world tile so the scene graph culls whole tiles for free. At 128 m tiles a 510 m reach is ~64 tiles, of which ~20 are in frustum -- about 20 draw calls against the 40-50 Quest 2 allows, and **zero** per-frame per-instance cost. Rewriting a tile's matrices costs 32 ns each and is paid only when that tile is rebuilt, one tile per frame (§6).

This is the regime §0 flagged as untested and guessed wrong about: it assumed batching's win was "thousands of cheap objects", and thousands of cheap objects is precisely where batching loses.

### And on a Quest 2 the crossover is not a crossover -- it is at zero

**Measured in the headset, on the grass bed, with nothing else in the scene and nothing else changed: 5 fps as a `BatchedMesh`, 50-60 fps as an `InstancedMesh` at three times the triangles.** That is not the 37 ns argument above scaled up. It is a different finding, and it overrides the rule for anything that ships to the headset:

> **On Quest 2, a** `BatchedMesh` **buys nothing.** Individual meshes for the handful of things the player is close to, `InstancedMesh` for everything scattered. Do not spend effort tuning a `BatchedMesh`, and do not treat a multi-geometry LOD ladder as a reason to keep one -- the ladder is the cheaper thing to give up.

**`MDRAW` reads `yes` on the Quest browser, so the fallback loop is not the explanation** -- that was the first suspect and it was wrong. `WEBGL_multi_draw` being *present* does not promise a *hardware* path: `glMultiDrawElements` is free to be a driver-side loop over the same tens of thousands of descriptors, and on a mobile tiler it evidently is. Two further costs are `BatchedMesh`'s alone and survive even a hardware multi-draw: the vertex stage resolves `getIndirectIndex(gl_DrawID)` and then fetches its matrix and colour out of data **textures**, which is vertex texture fetch on a chip that hates it; and `onBeforeRender` rebuilds the multi-draw arrays on the CPU **twice per frame in XR**, because `WebGLRenderer`'s XR path calls `renderScene` once per eye. The A/B could not separate which of the three dominates and did not need to: all three are gone together on the instanced path.

**What it costs to give up, honestly:** the LOD ladder (one geometry per mesh), per-instance frustum culling, and the sub-`count` skip for hidden instances. The last two are recoverable -- an `InstancedMesh` culls whole tiles for free and `count` still truncates the tail -- and a per-instance dissolve that used to ride on `BatchedMesh`'s colour alpha rebuilds on an ordinary `InstancedBufferAttribute` (`aPropFade`, `instancedFade` in `src/material.js`). The ladder is the real loss, and on the beds measured so far a far-field size ramp in the vertex shader has bought back more than the ladder did: see `GROW_SCALE` in `src/v2/render/grass.js`, where 2x cards at half the density are more coverage for half the triangles.

**Everything scattered has been converted except the rocks.** Grass, the forest, ferns, litter, mushrooms and dead wood all draw as `InstancedMesh`es; only the six rock beds are still `BatchedMesh` and still carry a ladder inside one call, so the per-instance CPU numbers in the tables above stand for them alone.

**Litter, mushrooms and dead wood went across last, and the price was their banks.** An instanced layer pays `tiers x variants` draw calls, so mushrooms' ninety variants would have wanted two hundred and seventy meshes. The banks collapsed to one shape per species and one size (5 species x 3 tiers = 15 meshes) and to a 4 m log and a 3 m stump (2 x 3 = 6); litter is one pebble on one mesh and needs no bank at all (§22). Where a layer does want per-instance pictures back, `texLayer` is a plain `attribute float` in the prop vertex shader, so `InstancedArena.addInstancedAttribute` can carry it per instance with no shader change -- the cheap route to per-variant bark, should it be wanted. Three batched draw calls became twenty-two instanced ones, bought with the loss of the `onBeforeRender` sweep -- which runs twice a frame in XR -- and of the vertex texture fetch.

**An `InstancedMesh` holding one instance is not free, and that is what decides where the collapse has to stop.** three's `renderInstances` early-returns at `primcount === 0`, so an EMPTY mesh never becomes a GL draw call -- but `renderBufferDirect` still pays it a render-list entry, a `setProgram` and a `bindingStates.setup`, per mesh per eye, before finding that out. The saving scales with instances per mesh; the cost is flat per mesh. A layer with one prop in it is therefore no cheaper instanced than as an ordinary `Mesh`, and slightly worse, which is why a bank collapse is a prerequisite for the move rather than tidying done afterwards.

**The forest kept its ladder by paying for it in draw calls rather than giving it up.** `src/v2/render/trees.js` draws **sixteen** `InstancedMesh`es -- four tiers by four species -- inside one `THREE.Group`, and a tree changing tier moves between meshes instead of changing a geometry id. Sixteen and not sixty-four because the size ladder is gone: one variant per species, and a placement varies it by a uniform 0.5x to 1.5x on the matrix, which the impostor cards need anyway (a uniform scale stretches the photograph evenly with the card). Each mesh is packed DENSE -- `count` is its live population and freeing a slot swaps the last one into the hole -- so a rim-dissolved tree costs nothing rather than a degenerate draw. **The cross-dissolve survived the move, at a price:** two tiers that are two different meshes cannot dither past each other, so the departing tier is held by a DUPLICATE instance in its own mesh for the quarter second, on the complementary threshold. That is one extra tree's triangles per swap in flight -- around 26 at a walking pace, ceilinged at 256 -- and past the ceiling a swap goes back to being a cut. All three boundaries dissolve, the 8 m one included: losing the limb barrels reads as loudly as losing the mesh (see *Distance tiering* below).

The fern rows are also the honest record of a shortfall: they are what a **0.46 ferns/m²** scatter produces close in and **0.02** past 26 m, not the 2 ferns/m² this document prices everywhere else. The binding cost is the rebuild, not the triangles -- every candidate cell in the disc pays a hash and most survivors pay a `heightAndSlopeAt`, and the cell count goes as `(radius / spacing)²`. Closing the gap by tightening spacing is quadratic in rebuild time and would blow the frame. That is why reach and density are two `Scatter` kinds over one geometry bank rather than one kind with a bigger radius: `fern` is dense to 26 m, `fern_far` is sparse to 80 m, and holding the near spacing all the way out would be thirteen times the rebuild. The fix for the density itself is that ferns grow in patches: scatter a few cluster centres and fill each one, which buys high local density for a fraction of the candidate cells. That is a placement change and it is not built.

Two things changed to make this fit, and both are decisions rather than tuning:

- **Terrain coarsened from a 1.2° triangle cap to 5.72°**, 371 drawn leaves to 71, 237k triangles to 45k. On Quest 2 the ground cannot have 68% of the frame, and this is the single largest triangle recovery available anywhere in the project -- 192k, more than the entire prop budget. `[` and `]` live tuning stays.
- **Instance count is now a budgeted quantity, not a footnote.** ~9,500 visible instances at 37 ns is 0.35 ms/frame desktop, ~1.4 ms on Quest 2, which fits. The far card bands are excluded from that figure because they are `InstancedMesh` and cost nothing per instance -- which is the only reason the row above them can afford 3,700 cards.

### Download budget -- a non-issue, which is the liberating part

- Texture arrays, 128×128 × ~150 layers, RGBA8: ~9.8 MB uncompressed, **~1.5 MB with ASTC 6×6**. See §9 -- compression is required, not optional
- ~80 assets × ~500 tris, meshopt-compressed: **~1-2 MB**
- World data: **0 bytes** (it is a seed)

**Under 10 MB total.** Go wide on asset variety. The constraint is `MAX_ARRAY_TEXTURE_LAYERS` and Meshy generation time, not bandwidth.

### Distance tiering

**Terrain LOD: one rule, one knob, one unit.** Quadtree chunks at a **constant** `CHUNK_RES` (16), splitting while `cell > range * tan(LOD.triDeg)` -- cell being the node's grid spacing in metres and range the 3D distance from the eye to its box. A length over a range is an angle, so the rule reads straight off the screen: **refine until no triangle looks wider than** `triDeg` **degrees.** Every visible triangle, underfoot or on the horizon, gets the same angular size, and it is bounded by construction rather than on average. Chunk size halves with depth; vertex *density* therefore doubles, but vertex *count* stays fixed -- which is what lets every chunk share one slot size in the batch. **Skirts** (vertical flanges at chunk edges) hide cracks between adjacent levels -- far simpler than stitching and invisible in practice. Fog and atmospheric desaturation hide popping and do most of the work of selling scale.

This replaced a raw distance test (`boxDistance < size * splitK`) plus a per-node elevation bias, which produced angular inversions -- near ground blockier than far ground, with a hard seam at quadrant boundaries. A per-node **geometric error** term was also built, measured and removed: this height field is fbm, so its roughness is scale-invariant, which makes a cell-size cap already an error cap. Both arguments are written out in full at the top of `src/terrain/quadtree.js`, including why raising `CHUNK_RES` to buy back angular error costs *more* triangles at equal quality rather than fewer. `triDeg` is live-tunable with `[` and `]` because it is a judgement call that has to be made looking at ridgelines in a headset.

**The cap is the terrain's entire share of the frame, and it is the biggest single lever in the project.** Two ceilings, not one: *selection* holds slot-pool entries and must never overrun `SLOT_COUNT` (768) because the current selection is exempt from eviction and overrunning throws; *drawn* is the part inside the 110° eye cone, and that is what spends triangles. Worst case over 606 positions × 4 headings, half of them airborne (`scripts/probe-trideg.mjs`, same cameras and cone as `check-sim.mjs` section 5):

| `triDeg` | selection + 21 pinned | drawn leaves | drawn tris | % of 350k |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1.2 (was the default) | 670 | 371 | 237k | 68% |
| 1.8 | 424 | 219 | 140k | 40% |
| 2.2 | 340 | 176 | 113k | 32% |
| 3.0 | 277 | 123 | 79k | 22% |
| 4.0 | 223 | 95 | 61k | 17% |
| **5.72** (ships) | **163** | **71** | **45k** | **13%** |
| 7.0 | 139 | 59 | 38k | 11% |
| 7.2 | 22 | 1 | 1k | 0% |

**5.72° ships.** It was picked by eye with the `[` `]` keys on a ridgeline and then measured, which is the right order for a knob whose whole purpose is a judgement about how chunky is tolerable. It is coarse and it is meant to be: the ground is the backdrop, and 192k triangles is more than the entire prop budget.

**10% of the frame is not reachable with this knob**, and the reason is a floor rather than a tuning failure. Below about 11% the cost stops being "how many splits" and becomes "how many chunks × 640 triangles each", and 640 is fixed by `CHUNK_RES` 16 -- 512 surface triangles plus **128 of skirt, 20% of every chunk drawn**. The levers that would actually reach 35k are `CHUNK_RES`, cheaper skirts, or a smaller `VIEW_HALF_ANGLE`; none of them is this knob, and none is worth spending before the ceiling itself is measured on the device.

**The 7.2° row is a cliff, not a data point, and** `MAX_TRI_DEG` **now stops short of it.** Range is floored at a node's own half-size, so for any node containing the camera the split test reduces to a constant:

> `cell / range = (size / CHUNK_RES) / (size / 2) = 2 / CHUNK_RES = 1/8`

Size cancels, so past `atan(1/8) = 7.125°` *no node containing the camera ever splits* and the entire 16 km world draws as one chunk with 1 km triangles. `MAX_TRI_DEG` was 8.0, which put that inside the reach of the `]` key -- the same class of bug `MIN_TRI_DEG` exists to prevent at the other end. It is now 7.0, and the ceiling moves if `CHUNK_RES` does.

`periphDeg` **is clamped up to** `triDeg` **at the point of use.** The periphery is a *coarser* target; the pair silently inverted the moment `triDeg` passed the 5.0 periphery, refining ground behind the player harder than ground in front of her. Measured at 5.72: 178 slots inverted, 163 clamped, identical drawn triangles. At the shipped cap the clamp binds and the grading does nothing, which is the better outcome anyway with the pool 79% empty -- and the grading resumes on its own if the knob goes back below 5.

Two levers exist besides the cap, and neither replaces it. `VIEW_HALF_ANGLE` (the streaming margin, 90°) buys slots almost for free -- 45% of the selection sits outside the eye cone and is GPU-culled per instance -- but it buys little in *drawn* triangles. **Per-eye stereo** is the other: three renders once per eye with no multiview, so this cost is paid twice, and it is why the ground could never have two thirds of the budget.

The pool is now heavily oversized for the default -- 163 slots used of 768 -- because `SLOT_COUNT` has to cover `MIN_TRI_DEG`, the finest the `[` key can reach. That is roughly 15 MB of vertex and index buffer held for a setting nobody ships. **Shrinking the pool means giving up the fine end of the knob**, which is a trade to make on the device, not before it.

**Triangles are not what binds, and neither are they what the card tier is for.** The prop budget is ~190k (350k ceiling − 45k terrain − ~115k everything else). Within that, the crossovers above are set by perception and by instance count, not by arithmetic on triangles -- pushing LOD0 out to where its triangles stop being worth it would put it past 400 m.

**Parallax is what binds.** A billboard's defect is not that it lacks detail -- a 128 px impostor carries more foliage than a 45-triangle decimated conifer does. Its defect is that it does not turn as you walk past it, and that error is an angle, `atan(depth / distance)`, which no triangle count touches. Under ~2° it stops reading as wrong at walking pace, which gives the rule the table above is built from:

> **billboard crossover ≈ prop depth ÷ tan(2°) ≈ depth × 28.6**

That is 120 m for a 4.2 m deep tree, 166 m for the v2 kit's median 5.8 m building (§19; the card is taken at the round 200 m just past it), 17 m for a 0.6 m boulder and **14 m for a 0.5 m deep fern**. The rule scaling with prop size is why the ladder is per class and not global.

The bush class takes its card at 26 m rather than the 14 m the rule permits, because the 12 m in between is bought by a 28-triangle LOD2 at a few hundred instances, which is cheap in every budget at once. The rule sets the point past which a card is *allowed*, not the point at which it is *required*.

**What shipped stops at 80 m, and neither parallax nor triangles is why.** The pixel argument below permits 170 m and the fill argument says the whole band is nearly free, but reach on a `BatchedMesh` scatter is bought in candidate cells: `(2 × radius / spacing)²`, each surviving cell paying a `heightAndSlopeAt`. `fern_far` at 5.0 m spacing and 80 m is 1,089 cells and ~2.4 ms of rebuild, which is already the cost class of every other kind in the world; the same reach at the near kind's spacing would be 19,500. So the current ceiling is a **rebuild** ceiling, and the two things that lift it are the ones the ladder above already names -- clump cards, which buy reach at a fraction of the cells, and moving the far band to tiled `InstancedMesh`, which takes it off the per-frame budget entirely. Until then 80 m is what a scatter that also has to place trees, rocks and grass in the same frame can afford.

**A single quad is only a billboard if something yaws it toward the eye, and the thing that does is a vertex shader** (`billboardVertex` in `src/material.js`). Not CPU matrices -- writing 22,000 instance matrices a frame is what makes people think billboards are expensive. The quad is authored in object space and the shader spins it about its own Y axis toward `cameraPosition` as it transforms it: zero CPU, no per-frame writes, and it composes with `BatchedMesh` and `InstancedMesh` alike because it happens before projection. Four details are load-bearing. It is **cylindrical, not spherical** -- pitching to face the camera lays a fern on its back and lifts its base off the ground. It **cancels the instance's own yaw** (taken from the batching matrix's first column), because the same instance is a randomly-yawed mesh up close and a billboard far away. It is selected by **texture layer against a per-material uniform list**, the same idiom `uSnowLayers` uses, because `BatchedMesh.addGeometry` throws on a geometry missing an attribute the arena has, so a new per-vertex flag would be a change to every generator in the project -- and per-material so the sparse v1 scatter, which draws these same layers as fixed crossed cards, opts out by not asking. And its **normal stays vertical rather than turning with the quad**: a normal that tracks the eye makes N·L a function of where the player stands, so the bed brightens and dims as they turn on the spot, and at this range a fern bed *is* a ground surface.

**The spin is wrong for any prop that has a HEADING, and dead wood is where that showed.** Cancelling the instance's yaw is exactly right for a fern or a stump, because those props are near enough radially symmetric that no yaw was ever visible; it is a lie for a fallen log, whose whole silhouette is a direction. A spun log card holds still against the eye while the mesh under it points along its own yaw, so every LOD swap looks like the log turning to a new heading and turning back the moment the player walks in again -- an animation the player caused, which is the worst class of LOD artefact. So a log's card is **fixed and carried by the instance's own yaw**: `buildImpostorCard` takes an `azimuth`, which turns the quad's plane to face where `bakeImpostor` stood its camera, and the card drops the `upNormal` marker `billboardVertex` reads so `propSpun` evaluates to zero for it. A snag on the same material keeps the spin, because a stump is a solid of revolution and turning its card costs nothing. The price is that a fixed card thins to a sliver seen end-on where the spun one showed a full-length log; both are wrong at that one angle and the fixed error is momentary rather than a motion. The fix if it ever reads badly is the folded pair the tree cards use, not a wider mesh band. `src/props/deadwood-bank.js`.

**Crossed quads where the scatter is sparse, one turning quad where it is dense.** Two fixed quads crossed over a half turn leave a worst case of 45° off a plane, which is what a card at ~20 px can carry, and cost 4 triangles against a 28-triangle LOD2; that is what v1's `fern_far` uses, and at its density the difference is a rounding error. The /v2 carpet is the opposite case: 97% of its instances are in the far tier, so whatever that tier costs each is what the layer costs. Measured over 9,098 ferns: **31k triangles with billboards against 247k holding LOD2 out**, and 49k with crossed cards. The billboard also takes the crossover from 26 m to **14 m**, which is what the parallax rule permitted all along -- v1 takes its card late only because it can afford to. The bake and the card builder are shared with the tree class in `src/props/impostor.js`; plane count and `upNormal` are per-class arguments, and the tree class runs both. Structure is the one class that does not share that builder, and the one class with **no spun tier at all**: its two planes stand on the building's own axes at their own two *widths*, which `buildImpostorCard` cannot express, and nothing spins because a spun quad shows one width from every angle -- a 4:1 error on a longhouse. It can afford to keep the cross to the cull distance for the reason the tree class cannot: a town is thirty instances, so the two triangles a billboard saves are sixty across the village. It is also the one class whose bake is keyed on **material rather than on instance**: 20 photographs, one per wall style x roof kind, each of a stand-in cottage, shared by every building wearing that pair -- 1.25 MB against 9.3 for one per variant, and the widths stay per building so the silhouette does not pay for it. §19 and `src/buildings/v2/card.js`. Trees take the billboard on the same arithmetic read the other way, and the split between the two card tiers is where the depth stops paying: the cross keeps 15-100 m, where a 9 m tree is still large enough on screen that binocular disparity across three planes reads and a flat cutout does not, and the billboard takes 100 m and out, where it does not -- which is 39,412 of the forest's 40,972 instances, so the six triangles a cross would cost out there are the 39k the single triangle gives back. That card is **one** triangle rather than two, apex up for pine and apex down for oak, birch and aspen, which is the shape each species' outline already is; it gives up two corners of the baked photograph, keeping 89% of pine's foliage against the real LOD0 silhouette, 84% of oak's, 74% of birch's and 85% of aspen's. The clipped shoulder is affordable only because the swap into that tier is a quarter-second cross-dissolve rather than a cut.

**Two impostor layers for all sixteen fern variants**, cut on `arch` (`fernCardLayer` in `src/props/fern-bank.js`). A layer has to earn itself by reading as *different at the range it is seen*: at 26 m a 0.55 m fern is ~20 px tall on Quest 2's default eye buffer, where an upright shuttlecock and a wide flat spray are still a silhouette apart and `fronds`, `pitch` and `taper` are not. Sixteen layers would be 1 MB to redraw two outlines eight times each. Both this and the plane count are arithmetic rather than looked-at judgements; `gen-fern.html`'s `card` button is where they get confirmed.

**The bush class shipped with three mesh tiers where the tree class has two, and the "why not a third tier" argument below does not reach it.** That argument is about trees: a third conifer tier installs a 45-triangle canopy with no silhouette in place of a 128 px impostor whose only defect is already under 2°, and the one cheap tree *canopy* that was worth building -- LOD1's twenty-triangle bundle -- was built, shipped and then withdrawn on looks, leaving the tree's second tier a wood-only coarsening that saves 31% rather than 85%. The bush class is the opposite case on both counts. Its tiers are not decimations of one mesh but *re-generations at a different segment count* -- 6, 4 and 2 segments per frond -- so a tier costs one more entry in a generated bank rather than a hand-authored asset, and the whole 48-geometry bank is 134 KB (the card tier adds 0.6 KB on top, being two geometries for all sixteen variants). And the thing the tiers control is the smoothness of a frond's arc, which is the fern's silhouette rather than a detail inside it. Judged in `gen-fern.html`: 4 → 6 segments is an obvious gain, 6 → 8 is close to undetectable. So the finest tier only has to reach as far as that arc is legible, which is why LOD0 stops at 5 m instead of the 12 m the class allows, and the two tiers behind it carry the rest for a third of the triangles. A single 6-segment tier out to 25 m costs 82k against this ladder's 42k at the same density.

**The rock class takes no card: its far tier is a mesh, the six-face hull T6 (`design/23-rock-generator.md`), and the argument that refused a card stands.** A rock impostor is photographed from the side, where a rock's normals are horizontal by construction, so the lean that makes a tree card read has nothing to bite on; and every attempt to hand a card the mesh's light, colour, dimensions and burial from every eye fell out of step with the mesh at one of them. The hull is the mesh's own box on five vertices, tinted, textured, lit and bedded by the code that draws every other tier, so nothing has to be matched. The area it covers is what matters: the tier it displaces is T20, and triangles are not what a distant rock costs -- a visible `BatchedMesh` slot is ~41.5 ns a frame whatever geometry it points at, and that number does not move -- but the giants bed runs to 1,250 m and everything past its far distance is the bulk of an annulus two square kilometres across. Every rock hands over at the same **angular** size rather than at the same distance, `ROCK_LOD_AT`'s last rung being 25 m per metre of ladder size, so a 25 cm cobble is the hull at 6 m and a 20 m block at 500, across a population whose sizes differ by a factor of eighty; put that edge in too close and you are looking at six faces from twenty metres. **The whole ladder came in by a factor of about 1.75 on measurement rather than on taste**: at the old bands a ridge camera was spending 110k triangles on rock, and moving the metre-scale beds to 15 / 40 / 80 -- 0.7° for a 1 m rock is 82 m, so those are that rule applied literally -- and scaling the same ladder by rock size for the larger beds brought it to 49k, a 55% cut with every bed contributing and no bed's angular handover pushed past where the one above it already sat.

**Nothing in the rock's shader spins, and `billboardVertex` is cylindrical for everything that does.** Every card in the world grows out of the ground, so keeping its height vertical is the truth rather than an approximation, and the cylindrical spin turns `(x, z)` about the instance's Y, which preserves each vertex's height and horizontal radius and so keeps every spun vertex inside the bounding sphere `BatchedMesh` culled it against. A rock has no up, and a rock on a slope is looked at from above, which is why its far tier is a mesh under the ordinary instance matrix: the bed rolls it, sinks it and tints it once, and the hull, the mesh tiers and the terrain cut each other in the depth buffer as any three meshes do -- see `design/25-rock-scatter.md`. `scripts/check-rock-far.mjs` draws one rock both ways under four suns, on the flat and bedded into a 40° hillside, and holds the hull's mean brightness and colour to the mesh's within a tenth and its covered ground to the mesh's within the bounds six faces can promise. **One ladder, and the thresholds are per rock rather than per bed or per size class.** The one shape builds T320, T80, T20 and T6, so `ROCK_BAND_COUNT` is 4 and the tier table is rectangular by construction. `ROCK_LOD_AT = [4, 7.5, 25]` is in **metres of camera distance per metre of the rock's ladder size**: a rock two metres tall holds T320 to 8 m, T80 to 15, T20 to 50 and is the hull past that; a twelve-metre spire holds T320 to 48 m and is the hull at 300.

**The ladder size is the LONGEST of the rock's three box axes.** `rockLodSize` returns `max(height, width, depth)`, and `/gen-rock` names which of the three won. It matters even with one shape, because the quarter turns lay that shape down: the boulder is 2.00 x 1.44 m in plan and 1.16 m tall, so which axis is vertical is a placement roll, and height alone would judge the same rock differently depending on which face it came to rest on. The longest axis is the only one of the three that cannot under-read a shape lying down, and under-reading is the failure that shows -- a rock that cards early is a rock that visibly goes flat while you are still looking at it. The scatter keeps the shape's size at scale 1 in `shapeLod` and multiplies by the instance's own `instScale`, so `update` compares `d²` against `size² × LOD_SQ[b]` for one multiply and no square root, and no second per-instance array. `instSpan` survives holding the instance's **footprint width**, which is the right metre for the anti-overlap dart and the wrong one for the ladder.

These thresholds are deliberately tighter than a pixel-error argument gives: holding a triangle above about three pixels wants roughly 70 m per metre before the hull, and this asks for 25. Measured by `check-rocks`'s three-world traverse, the beds draw **38,292 triangles over 6,742 rocks** on the endless cliff -- the world where the ordinary beds run flat out -- 10,986 over 7,657 on the ridge, and 44,048 over 8,628 on the endless lake, which is water in every direction for every bed's whole reach and so a ceiling rather than a measurement of anything shipped. What the size-relative form buys is exact and not merely measured: the hull is taken at a fixed 25 m per metre of ladder size, so a rock's longest axis subtends the same angle at the moment it goes to six faces whatever size the rock is -- 1/25 of a radian, about 35 px at 1080p on /v2's 70° vertical field. A per-bed band list could not say that; it was one distance for a 0.3 m stone and a 10 m block alike.

**Nothing is culled before it has been the hull, whole, for a while, and the guarantee is a floor on the RANK rather than a rule about distance.** The prop thinning gives every instance a cull distance of `fullRadius / rank` from the rank it drew, which is exactly what makes density halve as distance doubles -- and a rank knows nothing about how big the rock that drew it is, so a 10 m block in a bed whose `fullRadius` is 45 m could be handed a gone-distance of 37 m and dissolve while it was still an LOD2 mesh, having never reached T6 at all. `_rankOf` floors the rank at `fullRadius / _fadeFloor(sizeRoll)`, which makes the identity `fullRadius / rank = max(fullRadius / u, fadeFloor)` hold: the tail is still taken on the same law, it just starts past the rock's own far distance. `_fadeFloor` is `farGoneAt`: the far distance times `ROCK_FAR_LIFE` (1.0, the shortest that is still a band), divided by `FADE_BAND`, because `RimFade.place` takes the distance at which the instance is GONE and the rim fires short of it -- a floor at the far distance itself would have the hull arriving already dithering, indistinguishable from the finer mesh dithering out. The divisor stays the old 0.85 band even though the trigger is now `RIM_AT` = 0.925 of `gone`: dividing by the smaller number pushes the gone-distance further out, so the whole-hull band it buys is a superset of the one it promises, and swapping in `RIM_AT` would shave 8% off every rock's reach for nothing but tightness. The floor goes on in the first pass of `_growTile`, before the survivor test and the sort, so the tile ladder, the pool bound and the shader all read one scalar and tile stability is untouched; and it is an upper bound rather than a fit, since `instScale` is linear in the size roll on both branches and `lodP + roll·lodQ` therefore bounds every per-environment line. **A bed can still cut a rock short by simply ENDING first, and that is a separate check.** Past `radius` the tiles are not resident at all, so a bed whose reach is shorter than its own biggest rock's far distance culls that rock outright, mid-ladder. The constructor now refuses one (`minReach`), and it is what sets the longest reaches in the file: `embedded`'s 64 m block reaches T6 at 1,600 m and owes a `ROCK_FAR_LIFE` band past that, so nothing under 1,882 m is accepted and the bed runs 1,900. The thinning itself is switched off while the hand-over and the cull are told apart in the headset (`ROCK_THIN`, `design/25-rock-scatter.md`), so every rock now lives to its bed's `radius`. The extra ground is bought in hulls -- everything out there is six triangles. `check-rocks` asserts the whole of it per instance across every world it builds: none dissolving before it reaches T6, with the closest call's margin reported. It used to read the gone-distance back out of the colour texture, which was the stronger test -- it checked the number the shader sees rather than a restatement of the arithmetic behind it -- and cannot any more, because that slot holds only a clock now; so it reads `RimFade.gone` and gates the texture separately, for holding nothing but the never-fade sentinel or a stamp.

**The rim dissolve runs on a CLOCK, not on distance, because a smoothstep over distance is a permanent artefact for a fixed fraction of every scatter.** It used to be `1 - smoothstep(gone * FADE_BAND, gone, d)` in the vertex stage: free, stateless, and wrong in a way that is invisible in a still frame and unmissable in a standing one. Gone-distances are `fullRadius / rank` with rank uniform on [0,1), so at any distance `d` the drawn instances are those with `rank < fullRadius / d` and the ones inside their own fade band are `rank` in `(FADE_BAND * fullRadius / d, fullRadius / d]` -- a ratio of exactly `1 - FADE_BAND`, **15%, independent of d**. It is not a rim. Every distance in the world has 15% of its props parked at partial coverage forever, and standing still is the worst case rather than the safe one. `src/v2/render/rim.js` replaces it with the mechanism the LOD cross-dissolve already had: one trigger distance, a stamped quarter second, and a binary steady state. `RimFade` is owned one-per-batch by all seven scatters (trees, rocks, grass, ferns, litter, deadwood, mushrooms) and the fade slot now carries only a clock, which is what let the sign-packing in the paragraph below go away.

**The trigger goes at the MIDPOINT of the band the smoothstep used to span, and that is the one number this had to choose.** `RIM_AT = (1 + FADE_BAND) / 2` = 0.925. Cutting at `FADE_BAND` and cutting at `gone` are both defensible and both wrong by the same 16%: measured on the boot forest of 40,972 trees, 4,605 stand past `gone` and 14,766 past `gone * FADE_BAND`, so the band holds 10,161 trees -- **a quarter of the scatter** -- and a hard cut at either end thins or thickens the visible forest by a sixth. The old ramp is symmetric, so a cut at its midpoint preserves expected coverage: 9,880 hidden against an old effective 9,686, within 2%. The same identity is what makes the drawn bed predictable everywhere else -- an instance is drawn while `gone * RIM_AT > d`, which is the thinning law's own keep-fraction read at `d / RIM_AT`, and `check-grass` holds the strip carpet to that across four rings (2.98/2.98, 1.71/1.69, 0.66/0.65, 0.24/0.24 per m²). What it costs is that the outermost shell, `radius * RIM_AT` to `radius`, is now empty where it used to be drawn at falling coverage. What it buys back is the rest of the world not being stippled.

**A clock needs a sweep, and the sweep is grass's veil generalised rather than a new cost.** Distance was free per frame; a trigger has to be tested, so every scatter now walks its far instances -- 5 ns each, amortised over `RIM_PHASES` = 8 frames with each tile's phase derived from its *coordinates* rather than its position in the tile Map. That was already being paid for grass alone, and it hands every other scatter grass's own saving: instances past the trigger are set invisible and stay resident, so the grass carpet is 7,250 of 26,647 hidden and **23.4% of the bill recovered** (62.0k resident, 47.5k drawn). Being late to *hide* costs triangles; being late to *show* is a pop, so the hide and show thresholds both carry a slack sized to the camera's decaying peak speed, a rising slack forces an immediate sweep, and the show test is 2 m inside the hide test so a camera on the boundary cannot flicker. The failure this can still have is a prop that outruns the ramp -- crossing `gone * (1 - RIM_AT)` inside `PROP_FADE_SECONDS`, which is 21 m/s for a grass tuft and 450 m/s for a tree.

**And the LOD swap itself is a cross-dissolve, on the same ONE float per instance that the rim dissolve rides on.** A rock crossing `ROCK_LOD_AT` used to cut from one tier to the next in a frame, which is a different pop from the one the rim answers -- the rim is about a prop *leaving*, and this is a prop staying and changing shape. Rocks, grass, ferns and the forest all run it (`_crossFade` / `_endFade` / `_sweepFades` in `src/v2/render/rocks.js`, `grass.js`, `ferns.js` and `trees.js`, near enough the same sixty lines in each because their pools are the same `free`/`_thin`/`_release` structure; `design/attic/lod-cross-fade-extraction.md` is the case for lifting it out, and the reason the bigger "one pool class for every scatter" abstraction is not the cut to make): pull a spare instance out of the pool, copy the original's matrix and tint into it, give it the OLD geometry, and stamp both halves with the current prop clock -- the ghost as departing, the original as arriving. **The channel this rides on is `BatchedMesh`'s per-instance colour alpha**, and it now carries one kind of thing only: **a negative, biased clock reading** while a transition is in flight, and **1.0** -- three's own default, meaning never fade -- the rest of the time. It used to carry a positive gone-distance as well, which is what made the rim a smoothstep on distance; see below for why that went. The biases (`FADE_OUT_BIAS` 1, `FADE_IN_BIAS` 4096) sit either side of the clock's 1024-second wrap so the departing range and the arriving range cannot meet, and Float32 at 5120 still resolves half a millisecond. The two halves take **complementary** thresholds against the same interleaved-gradient noise -- the arriving half flips `fadeT` -- so coverage is conserved exactly: every pixel of the silhouette is owned by precisely one tier at every instant, and the outline neither thins nor doubles. `check-trees` proves that by simulating the fragment test over 21 ramp positions x 64 dither levels and asserting zero holes and zero doubles. **The forest pays more for it than the others do**, because its tiers are sixteen separate `InstancedMesh`es: a tree cannot be in two of them at once, so the ghost is a second instance in the departing tier's own mesh rather than the same instance wearing an old geometry id. That means a third ceiling only the rock beds share -- a mesh that fills THROWS, so `PropArena.roomAt` is asked before the duplicate is taken -- and it means the tier caps carry `FADE_MESH_RESERVE` of headroom they are never sized for. The rock beds keep the ghosts to the headroom their tier caps already carry (`ghostRoom`, the cap less its population's share), because a ghost that took a population slot leaves the next REAL arrival to throw, and the gate teleports far enough with a still clock to prove it. All three boundaries get it, the 8 m one included: the limb barrels leaving is as visible as the mesh becoming a card.

**The ramp is a quarter second and it is EASED, and the ease is the fix for a complaint about latency that was not latency.** Coverage being conserved is what makes the dissolve invisible in the wrong way at the start of it: at 10% through, the arriving tier is isolated single pixels scattered over a still-solid silhouette, so a linear ramp spends its first fifth looking like nothing has happened -- at the old 500 ms window that is ~150 ms of dead air after the crossing, which reads as the animation being slow to start rather than as a swap in progress. Both levers were pulled. `PROP_FADE_SECONDS` is **0.25**, and `fadeP = fadeP * (2 - fadeP)` front-loads what is left: 19% of the swap has arrived after the first 25 ms against 10% linear, and half of it inside the first 75 ms. Any remap is safe here provided both halves get the same one, and they do -- there is one `fadeP` in `FADE_VERTEX` and the split below it is `fadeIn ? -fadeP : 1 - fadeP`. The shader's rate is interpolated out of `PROP_FADE_SECONDS` itself rather than written as a second literal, and `check-trees` asserts that, the window's ceiling, and the ease's shape (monotone, f(0)=0, f(1)=1, and the two anti-lag promises as numbers). **What bounds the cost is a pool ceiling, and past it the swap degrades to the pop it replaced.** A dissolve costs one extra visible instance for a quarter second; measured over thirty seconds walking at 5 m/s across the endless cliff apron that is 50 in flight in the mean frame, 1,151 in the worst and 2,366 ghost triangles at the peak against the ~99k the beds were drawing anyway. `FADE_MAX_INFLIGHT` is 1,024 per rock bed and 256 in the forest (where a duplicate is a whole tree), and is reachable only by teleporting or by flying -- `check-trees` fills the forest's at 60 m/s and a walk peaks at 26 -- `check-rocks` steps the camera 9 m between updates and saturates the densest bed exactly there, which is the intended failure: a beam that re-tiers a whole bed at once cuts, and walking never does.

**The hull's dimensions are the mesh's, on every axis and at every bearing, and that is the whole of the match.** Its box is `measured` exactly -- floor corners at the box's floor diagonal, the crown at `measured.height`, the shoulders pulled in so its plan area and its side silhouette areas are the reference's own -- so a rock going to six faces neither swells, shrinks nor moves, and it presents the mesh's own aspect from every eye, a top view included, because it is a solid and not a picture of one. What it gives up is roundness: a rock the bed has sunk to its shoulders shows a wedge where the mesh shows a dome, and on a 40° hillside that is two thirds of the mesh's ground over a row of them and a third on the rock that shows least -- a few pixels at 25 m per metre, and the one visible cost of the tier.

**The bank is ONE closed boulder, and the variety is made at placement instead.** `src/props/rock-bank.js` builds a single shape -- `BOULDER`, which is `ROCK_DEFAULTS` and a single authored seed, 2.00 x 1.44 m in plan and 1.16 m tall -- as T320/T80/T20/T6, and the cap plate beside it: **646 triangles and 76 KB for the whole bank**, against 15,791 triangles and 1,844 KB for the 25-variant bank it replaces. What carries the variety is `render/rocks.js` (§25): one of sixteen quarter turns about X and Z, a free yaw, a ±15° jitter, a size roll spanning 0.25 m to 20 m, a burial roll from a tenth to nine tenths off a floor that deepens to a third when the turn stands the rock on end, and a per-instance tint pulled toward the ground it stands on. **This is the change the whole rock chapter turns on**: one shape means one geometry ladder and no per-instance shape index anywhere in the scatter, the readout or the gate.

**The rock ships CLOSED, and that is a decision rather than an omission.** The boulder leaves `sit` at 0, so its underside is the displaced sphere's own surface and the bedding comes entirely from the scatter's burial roll. Turn `sit` up and the cut face becomes a horizontal disc at the bed plane -- triangles facing straight down at ground level that are never once seen, about a tenth of a T80's faces at `sit` 0.3 and half at 0.6, and free to drop, since the rim vertices are shared with the sides. That saving is refused whichever way the dial sits, because an open shell is only ever safe on something bedded and there is one rock: six beds place it at every burial from a tenth to nine tenths, on flat ground and on a 72-degree face, so no orientation can be promised and the failure -- looking into the inside of the rock through its backfaces -- is the loudest one in the world.

**Placement gained a RELIEF test on top of the four environments, because two families are defined by where in the country you stand rather than by what country it is.** The environment comes from water, then elevation, then slope, and none of the three separates the ground at the base of a cliff from the ground on its top: both are flat. `RockBed._relief` takes a **second difference along the fall line** -- walk 16 m up the local gradient and 16 m down it, and compare each against what the local slope alone predicted -- and returns `'foot'` when something steep stands above you, `'brow'` when the ground falls away below you, and null on a uniform slope of any steepness, which scores zero at both ends. Seven metres of departure over sixteen is the threshold, which at the 34° cliff line means something close to vertical. Variants carry an optional `site` tag and a site-tagged variant is held out of the ordinary pools entirely, so it can never turn up in the middle of a meadow: `talus` and `rubble` are `foot`, `lip` and `spire` are `brow`, and 75% of the rocks at a qualifying site take the site's own shapes. A foot site additionally runs *denser* than its environment, by a smooth value-noise clump field on a 26 m lattice against the boulder bed's 28 m tile, so a drift is about one tile across -- the lattice is seeded separately and is not aligned to the tile grid, so a pile never inherits the tile's edges, but nor does it span several of them. **That multiplier is capped at 1, and the cap is why the bed's numbers look redundant.** `envDensity` is an accept *rate*, so `envDensity * (1 + CLUMP_GAIN * clump)` saturates for any candidate the clump favours enough, and a saturated candidate silently throws the rest of its multiplier away -- which made `CLUMP_GAIN` a partly dead knob and left the cliff-foot doubling under-delivering at 1.85x. The fix is that `density` and `envDensity` only ever appear as a product: raising `density` 2.5x and dividing every `envDensity` by 2.5 leaves open ground identical to the digit while giving the clump 2.5x the headroom under the cap. The share of the field that saturates went 57% → 23%, which made `CLUMP_GAIN` a live knob again and took cliff feet to 1.98x the open ground beside them.

**But the ratio was never what was wrong, and a bed of its own is what fixed it.** 1.98x the open ground is still a rock every 10.6 m: a hillside with rocks on it, not the dense scree pile at the base of a cliff that was asked for. The absolute figure is the thing the eye reads and the ratio is not, which is also why the gate that measured the ratio stayed green through the whole of it. And no multiplier could have fixed it, for the reason above -- `envDensity` is an accept *rate*, so a candidate at a cliff foot was already being accepted at 100% and there was nothing left to raise. **Density past saturation can only be bought by offering more CANDIDATES, and candidates are per-bed.** So `scree` is a bed of its own: `footOnly`, so it exists nowhere but at the base of a face; **2.0/m²** against the boulder bed's 0.0105; and a `radius` of 140 m instead of 460, which cuts the area it has to cover elevenfold. 140 and not less, though less was tried: reach is the only thing it buys -- near-field spacing is identical at 140, 110 and 90, because thinning grades everything past the full radius away -- and at 110 the gate's traverse places no scree at all where 140 places 97, because the feet it passes sit in that band. Cutting it does not thin a pile, it deletes whole piles. **The target is one rock per metre of cliff foot averaged over ALL of it**, and the averaging ground is the load-bearing half: the complaint was swathes of foot with nothing on them, so a figure taken over the drifts alone would be scored on the ground that was never the problem. `check-rocks.mjs` reads 3,918 rocks over 3,912 m² of foot -- **one every 1.0 m**, median 1.44 m across, 83% of them between 0.5 and 3 m, and 28% of foot ground clear inside 1.5 m. The 12% of foot with nothing inside 3 m is the drift structure rather than a shortfall in density: random placement at this rate would leave essentially none. **`clumpFloor` is a look knob and not a cost knob**, which is the trap in it: at 0.42 it rejects 40% of candidates on four position hashes before the terrain sample, but measured over eight seeds the samples paid *per surviving rock* go **up** as it rises (14.3 at 0.42 to 17.5 at 0.65), because the floor removes rocks slightly faster than it removes queries -- so you cannot buy back the cost of a higher density by raising it, and raising it to buy bunching trades away exactly the coverage the bed was asked for: at density 2.0 the foot bare inside 1.5 m goes 28% → 36% → 46% → 52% across floors 0.42 / 0.50 / 0.58 / 0.65, and bare inside 3 m goes 12% → 23%. The width of a drift is `CLUMP_CELL`'s 26 m, not this number. What the density costs, honestly: ~200 ms of one-time `place()` against the 30 ms this bed used to cost, and a pool of 113,203 instances against 17,039, of which the gate's traverse actually uses ~3,083 -- `_poolBound` is position-blind by necessity, because a tile can be entirely inside a drift and entirely at a foot, and running dry throws, so the bound cannot be tightened by guessing at an average. **81% of that placement is `_relief`, not the terrain sample** -- four more field samples at ~4.3 µs the set, paid on every candidate that clears both the floor and the slope test and then thrown away for 91% of them when the site turns out not to be a foot, against `scatterAt`'s 18%. Memoizing it on the `PLACEMENT_CELL` grid would take the bed from ~200 ms to ~80 ms, because the count of distinct cells it probes plateaus near 2,100 whatever the density -- and it is offered rather than done, because candidate positions are not quantised, so the memo would reclassify candidates near a foot or brow edge and that is a change to what the world looks like rather than a free win.

**A fifth bed, `embedded`, and what forces it is `sinkRange` -- with the slope limit riding along.** Both are per-bed and neither can be varied within one. `SINK_DEEP` caps every other bed's burial roll at 0.8 with floors at 0.1, so no rate on an existing bed reaches the 0.7-0.9 this one exists for. And every other bed refuses ground past 42 to 62 degrees, because a rock *on* a vertical face is a rock falling off it -- so every bed's `cliff` rate was only ever being spent on the 34-to-42-degree apron and never on the face itself. **That is why the faces were smooth.** `embedded` runs at **72 degrees** with `allowSubmerged`, which is one job seen twice: a cliff face and a lake floor are both surfaces that need bumps, and a block let 70 to 90% *into* the surface is what gives a surface bumps out of the same closed mesh everything else uses. It is the file's highest `tilt` (0.9) and its only `sinkNormal` bed, since on a steep face a world-Y sink slides a rock down the slope rather than into it. `forest` is held at 0 not because it is unreachable but because an embedded block in flat woodland is a boulder sunk too far, which the boulders bed already covers.

**A sixth bed, `sunken`, and what forces this one is that a lake floor in relief is a texture rather than a place.** Blocks bedded flush into the floor *are* the floor, so however many there are the eye reads one continuous rippled sheet with the water as a shader over ground. What that floor has none of is a **silhouette**: something closed and rounded, standing proud, occluding what is behind it, resolving out of the murk before the surface it stands on does. `sunken` is that, at **0.5-5 m**, one every 100 m². Sparse is the design: something you come across is an event, and something there is one of every four metres is gravel. It carries a flag of its own, **`submergedOnly`**, which is `footOnly` for water -- needed rather than convenient, because `_envAt` hands the `river` environment to any ground within `SHORE_RISE` of the surface, so the dry shingle beside a lake is `river` too and an ordinary `envDensity` entry would furnish the beach as readily as the bed. The water test in `_growTile` therefore runs in **both directions** off one lookup: a bed either refuses submerged sites, or requires them, or -- which is most of them -- never pays for the lookup at all. The constructor refuses `submergedOnly` without `allowSubmerged`, since that pair places nothing and does it silently. Reach is 320 m, set by the same `minReach` check as every other bed, which is further than this water is clear anyway.

**The smallest stones are not rocks: they are the litter layer.** The `underfoot` bed put a modelled pebble on the ground every 1.7 m across every wood and cliffside at about 11 cm across -- forty-one specks for every rock big enough to read as a rock -- and what killed it was never the triangles: a `BatchedMesh` instance costs the same whether it is a four-face pebble or a seven-metre lip, and the bed carried a tier ladder, a card band and a 120 m reach for stones that are invisible past 15. `src/v2/render/litter.js` lays the same stones as one closed T20 pebble on an `InstancedMesh`, full density inside 8 m and thinned out to 28, six per-instance dials (yaw, size skewed small, stretch, flatness, burial, tint) standing in for a bank, and no ladder at all -- twenty triangles at every range, so the whole layer is a few tens of thousands of triangles and its cost is the instance count the panel's `litter` row shows. The riverbed went the same way: the `river` rate on the rock beds is **0.08**, one boulder per 36 m² to step around, with the litter's wet second pass carrying everything finer. §22 has the argument, the density table, where each environment rate caps and why the pebble is sunk rather than lifted.

**The ONE way a bed expresses size is `sizeByEnv`.** It is a range in **metres per environment**, divided back through the shape's `measured.width` at placement, and it is what a bed says instead of a multiplier, because one bed's job changes with the ground it is standing on: `embedded` is 3-20 m on a cliff face and 2-10 m on a lake floor. **Those numbers are not free in the same way, which is the thing to know before reaching for either.** `hi` in the bed constructor is the max over every reachable environment, so a band strictly under the widest one costs nothing at all -- the river range moves anywhere below the cliff's top and only the lake floor changes. The widest band is the one `radius` is sized from, and reach is quadratic: `embedded`'s 20 m top is what forces its 1,350 m reach, and doubling any bed's top doubles its reach and quadruples the ground it has to fill. It is affordable the moment the count pays for it -- a quarter the `envDensity` at twice the size holds both the bill and the fraction of the face under stone flat, and only the grain gets coarser -- but that is a trade and not a knob. **The consequence is that the authored `size` in `rock-bank.js` does not control world size at all** -- it is a default for the bench and a statement of what the shape is *meant* for, since both the metre ranges and the LOD thresholds read the resolved world width. `scripts/check-rocks.mjs` asserts the shipped metre ranges over the whole placed population for exactly that reason -- a bank edit that silently reproportioned every distant rock would otherwise be green everywhere.

**A rock takes its HUE from the ground it stands on, and its light from that ground's own vertices.** `rocks.js` pulls each instance's tint toward the terrain's vertex colour underfoot, from the chunk mesher's exported `shade()`, renormalised to unit luminance so what survives is hue and not magnitude -- the same construction `render/ferns.js` uses at a flat 0.35, but per environment and, everywhere, **stronger**: `GROUND_CUE` is `{ river: 0.75, cliff: 0.55, peak: 0.55, forest: 0.45 }`. The argument for keeping it under the ferns' figure -- a rock genuinely is a different material from the ground it sits on where a fern is growing out of it -- is true of the rock you can walk up to and false of nearly every rock on screen. Past `ROCK_LOD_AT`'s 25 m a rock is a six-face hull with no facets to read, so what is left of it *is* the hue, and a grey chip that does not belong to the ground under it is the entire reason the swap is visible at all. The ordering is by how much the stone owes the ground rather than by distance: a riverbed cobble is sorted and wetted by the water it lies in, a cliff or a scree slope is the mountain's own broken face, and a boulder in a wood is the one that is genuinely foreign -- it was carried there. None reaches 1, which would delete the bank's own colour and leave the stone a tinted lump of terrain; `check-rocks` holds the ordering, the finiteness of every entry (a missing environment writes a NaN into the colour texture) and the resulting luminance floor at 1.73x the ground's. Throwing the magnitude away is the load-bearing half: a rock's tint is a *destination* (`ENV_TINTS` says what the stone averages out to once the gain has white-balanced the photograph), so multiplying it by the terrain's near-black palette would not darken the rock, it would delete it, and what was actually wrong was that a neutral grey against saturated forest green reads as a cutout. The other half of "shade a rock like the ground it stands on" -- a rock the sun is not reaching -- was never this file's: `src/v2/main.js` patches the rock material with `lighting.patch({ mode: 'vertex' })`, so every rock already takes the terrain's own sun and sky shadow per vertex.

**Both prop masks run on blobs rather than on a bare octave of noise, and moss's blob IS the colony.** A single octave of value noise on a cubic lattice thresholds into rounded *squares* -- every blob is about one lattice cube across and the eye finds the grid long before it finds the noise -- so `blobField()` in `src/material.js` bends the lookup by a domain warp -- 1.9 lattice cells for snow, `MOSS_WARP` 3.2 for moss, the one of the three numbers that is per caller -- and then expands what comes back about its own midpoint by 1.55, which perturbs the *contour*, the only part of the field a threshold can see. The warp replaced a second octave at 2.07x and 28% weight that was doing the same job: at nearly two cells the displacement varies enough along a rim to break it up unaided, and the octave cost both a third noise evaluation and, worse, a NARROWER field, since a weighted sum of two near-independent samples pulls in toward the mean exactly where the cut has to live. The contrast expansion buys the spread back and is why the field now reaches both ends of [0,1] rather than 0.061 to 0.952. Two noise evaluations against one still makes it the most expensive thing in the fragment shader, so both call sites gate it on the distance fade being worth anything at all. `MOSS_FREQ` is 6.0, ~17 cm patches against snow's 16 cm, so about a dozen lobes wrap a 2 m boulder. A field four times finer covers the same fraction of the rock but spreads it as small stains everywhere, which the eye integrates into an even green wash; what says *grew here* rather than *painted on* is the bare stone BETWEEN the lobes, and that needs lobes big enough to have a between. The blob does still compete with the photograph inside it for the same band of detail -- snow's blobs *are* the snow, where moss's are only the shape of the stain -- which is why the shape below the patch size comes from WARPS rather than from a finer field. There are two of them, and a warp is the one thing that lengthens a contour for free -- it slides sample points about, so the distribution of values coming back is unchanged and every promise fitted against it survives. `MOSS_WARP` 3.2 cells bends the whole lobe, pulling runs out of it; `MOSS_FRAY` 0.45 at `BLOB_FRAY_FREQ` 5x then drags the *already-warped* point, so the detail reads as fraying ON a tendril rather than as a second wobble beside it, and at over twice its own wavelength it FOLDS the domain and pinches off islands -- flecks running out of the tendrils. Measured against no fray on a slice through the field: rim length per unit area up **59%** at a load of 0.35 and 49% at 0.6, coverage moving under 0.01, and the logit fit actually improving (worst 0.013 against 0.020). The fray costs a THIRD noise evaluation, so it carries its own fade at **3-9 m** against the lobes' 12-40: a fray feature is 3.3 cm, a fifth of a lobe, so by the rule that set the lobes' fade it stops resolving five times nearer -- and `blobField` skips the evaluation entirely once that fade reaches zero, which is what keeps the extra cost to boulders you could touch. **And moss takes the top first, the same half snow does**, `MOSS_UP` 0.35 against snow's 0.65, so the two are told apart by weight and by grain rather than by direction -- a firm white cap over a ragged green one that has already run well down the sides. Leaning harder is what a rock's flat faces forbid: `up` is constant across a facet, so a heavy lean flips one as a unit and the mask runs along facet edges as a seam. Moss's edge is a fixed soft width rather than snow's one-pixel `fwidth` cutover, because snow has a rim you can put your hand on and moss has none -- it thins out to flecks in the pits of the stone -- and it is deliberately ASYMMETRIC, `smoothstep( cut - MOSS_BLEND, cut + MOSS_BLEND * MOSS_BLEND_SKEW )` at 0.14 and 0.3, long on the way in and short on the way out so a patch keeps a body and grows a ragged skirt instead of blurring evenly; the thin end darkens to `MOSS_FRINGE` 0.78 as well as thinning, because sparse moss is flecks down in pits and a pit is in shadow, so a margin that only thins reads as a pale wash of green sitting on top of the rock. **The cut is a LOGIT, and that is what makes the moss slider mean anything.** Sweeping a threshold linearly assumes `creep` is spread evenly over its range and it is not -- it piles up around its median, sitting between 0.23 and 0.77 for eight tenths of a boulder's surface -- so the old linear pair spent most of its travel outside the range where creep lives, a load of 0.2 covered 0.2% of the rock, and setting the world's range to a plausible-sounding 0 - 0.5 bought a forest full of bare stone. Coverage at a given cut IS the complementary CDF of creep, so the cut yielding coverage c is creep's (1 - c) quantile, and that CDF is very nearly logistic: `MOSS_CUT_MID - MOSS_CUT_WIDTH * log(c / (1 - c))` at 0.550 and 0.134 makes the load read as the literal FRACTION of the surface that turns green (0.1 paints 0.087, 0.3 paints 0.307, 0.5 paints 0.500, 0.85 paints 0.870) and hands over both end promises for free, the cut running away above every creep there is at load 0 and below the floor at load 1. **Both constants are measured, and each is tied to something outside itself**: the width is creep's logistic scale so it belongs to `blobField`'s spread and any change to the contrast, the warp or the octave count invalidates it -- though not the frequency, which samples the same field at another scale and leaves its distribution alone; the mid belongs to the mask's edge, because an asymmetric `smoothstep` has its transition midpoint `MOSS_BLEND * (1 - SKEW) / 2` BELOW the cut and a mid fitted against a symmetric edge runs coverage a third high through the middle of the range. The prop program's cache key moved to `prop-moss-v5` with it.

**And each rock rolls its own ceiling for both seasons, as does each tree.** `setSnowVary(lo, hi)`, `setMossVary(lo, hi)` and `setLeafSnowVary(lo, hi)` take a *range* rather than a value, rolled off a hash of the instance root's world XZ, because there is still no free per-instance channel -- `BatchedMesh`'s colour RGB is the stone tint and its alpha is the fade distance. All three default to (1,1), a no-op, so `/gen-rock` and `/gen-tree` are unchanged and a slider there means exactly what it says; `Rocks.syncBands` sets snow to 0.3-0.5 and moss to 0-0.5 in the world, and `Trees.syncSnowLine` caps a canopy at 0.25-0.6. The snow cap is the one that had to exist: stone leans on the surface normal twice as hard as foliage does, so a full load takes the undersides too, and a rock at snow 1.0 is not a snowy rock, it is a white rock. **A canopy needed the same argument for a different reason.** The snow line is a property of the mountain, so every tree above it took the identical full ceiling and a stand went white in lockstep -- one poured material rather than weather that fell on individual trees. Neither end of the leaf range is round: 0 would be a hole in the weather, and at 1 a spruce and a birch are the same white blob, which throws away the only thing the four-species bank buys at distance. All three rolls are gated in the vertex shader, on *three different* lists, and they are easy to collapse into each other by mistake: snow takes `rockV` over `SNOW_HARD_LAYERS` (stone plus the four wood layers -- "does this wear the rock recipe", which grew when timber joined), moss takes the narrower `stoneV` over `SNOW_ROCK_LAYERS`, which is stone alone, and the canopy cap takes `leafV` over `SNOW_LAYERS`, which is the nine tree-foliage layers -- the five cuts plus the four tree impostors -- and deliberately neither grass nor fern. That last one has to be a real membership loop and not the complement `1.0 - min(rockV, 1.0)`, which reads true for grass, fronds, terrain and buildings alike. Neither is `MOSS_LAYERS`: that list says where moss may grow at all and is five layers wide, where these two ask which surfaces a per-instance *range* was chosen for. Gating the moss roll on the wider list is what put moss on timber and, by making the ceiling reachable on surfaces the fragment mask then refused, is half of why the forests came out with no moss on the rocks at all.

**Grassing a REGION is scattered strips; grassing a POINT is the 3-card clump.** A strip is one flat card several metres long whose `u` runs 0..tiles, so the atlas wrap draws the same grass cutout 3 to 6 times across its own length: one instance is 2 triangles and 3 to 6 clumps of grass. That is the canonical, default way to grass a field, a meadow or a hillside -- `style: 'strips'` in `src/v2/render/grass.js`, geometry from `buildGrassStripBank()` in `src/props/grass-bank.js`. The clump is the other case: three quads crossed at 60° so it reads solid from every azimuth, which is what a tuft at the side of a road, beside a doorway or at the foot of a wall wants (`buildGrassBank()`, `style: 'tufts'`). It is kept deliberately rather than deprecated -- but **nothing places grass by the point yet**, so the only thing that instantiates it today is the reference carpet the comparison below and `scripts/check-grass.mjs` are built on. A strip clump and a near clump card are the same plant, 0.64 m wide by 1.00 m tall at the mean, which the gate holds.

**The strip bed is 32% of the clump carpet's triangles for the same meadow, and most of that comes from thinning earlier rather than from the instance being flat.** Measured at a flat site, 70 m draw radius, 3 instances/m² at full density: 8,500 drawn strips and **17.0k triangles** against 22,290 drawn clumps and **53.4k**. The instance is the smaller half of that -- 2 triangles flat against a ladder averaging 2.39, so 0.84x -- and most of the saving is density, because on top of the shared `FULL_RADIUS / d` law the strip bed divides by 2 from 8 m and by 3.5 from 20 m (`STRIP_THIN`). Instances per square metre, strip bed against clump carpet: 2.99 vs 3.02 inside 8 m, 1.82 vs 3.02 to 20 m, 0.75 vs 2.00 to 40 m, 0.31 vs 1.06 to 70 m. It can start thinning at 8 m where the clump carpet cannot because a strip has one tier and no LOD ladder to be caught half-built -- which is the same argument as the paragraph below, read the other way.

**The grass clump does take a card, and the reason is the one the tri-count argument misses.** The obvious read is that a card saves nothing there: a tuft is 3 quads and its card is 1, so the ladder spans 6 → 2 triangles and cutting 4 triangles off an instance is not a saving worth a pop. That was the shipped position and it was wrong about which quantity was scarce. At 3 tufts/m² the far tier holds 86% of the instances, so those 2 triangles each are **46k of a 190k prop budget**, and the tuft's 0.55 m depth puts the parallax crossover at 16 m, well inside where the thinning starts. The clump style runs 3 / 2 / 1 planes with the last spun in the shader, for **62k triangles over 26,647 tufts against 108k** holding the 2-plane tier out (47.5k of that actually drawn, after the rim below). Measured by `scripts/check-grass.mjs`, which gates the ladder, the thinning law, the fade distances and the 0.5–1.5 m size range.

**The 3-plane tuft stands on a triangle, not an asterisk, and its cards are twisted 22°.** An impostor card is N quads crossed through one axis, which is right for a stand-in for a single subject -- every plane passes through the thing it is standing in for. It is wrong for a *clump*, where the crossing point is a seam that every plane's densest alpha stacks into, and the tuft reads as a plant on a pole. Putting the three cards on the *edges* of an equilateral triangle inscribed in the same 0.55 m footprint circle spreads that alpha across the footprint and buys ~0.1 m² of plan area for nothing: the cards become chords, so each is `width·√3/2` = 0.476 m rather than 0.55, and the instance is still 6 triangles. Independently, rotating each card's top edge 22° about the tuft axis breaks the vertical-slab silhouette a straight card has -- the UVs are explicit per-vertex, so the art twists with the corner instead of shearing. Both are free of the impostor bake, because `bakeImpostor` frames to the card's inflated extents and does not crop to alpha, so a narrower subject simply photographs narrower and draws back at true world size, and a rotation about the tuft axis cannot move a vertex off its own circle. **At two planes or fewer the cards stay crossed on the centre**: a 2-gon is degenerate, and two parallel chords share a killer azimuth where both go edge-on at once and the tuft disappears, which crossed cards never do.

**The tuft's foot is frayed into the texture at load, and the line it breaks is the terrain's, not the card's.** `PLACEMENT.sink` buries 0.04 m of a 0.55 m card at *any* instance scale, so the terrain cuts every tuft in the world at the same v = 0.927, and that identical row -- the picture's darkest, at 45–52% opacity against 4–20% higher up -- is the flat line the eye finds. A fray confined to the card's own bottom edge is work done underground, which is how the first attempt failed: at `top = 0.87` all but seven texels of the band were buried and the hem survived. `GRASS_FRAY.top = 0.74` cuts the deepest column 25 texels clear of the ground line, 21.5% of the tuft's opaque texels, and leaves 33 of 58 at that line in clumps rather than a row. Two terms decide each texel and they do not average: the column's own cut height comes from two octaves of value noise, **rank-normalised** (sort the columns, assign `rank/(width−1)`) because summing octaves piles the values around the mean and un-normalised the foot collapses from 12 distinct heights to 5; then the texel's own brightness **lifts** it from there toward the card's foot, so a lit blade reaches the bottom of the square in any column and the dark between blades goes. That brightness is ranked **per row, not per picture** -- the band darkens as it descends (90th-centile luminance 0.71 at v = 0.8, 0.44 at 0.96), so against one fixed window nothing at the bottom ever clears the bar and the fray shortens the card instead of opening it. Every eaten texel is flooded white on the way out for the same reason `tools/trees/layers.py` does it -- mips average RGB ignoring alpha, and a dark eroded texel bleeds the line straight back at distance. **Done to the layer rather than in the fragment shader** on two counts: grass is ~45% of the world's rasterised pixels, so a per-fragment fray recomputes a static decision ~2M times a frame, and shaping `LAYER.GRASS_TUFT` before the array upload means `bakeGrassImpostor` photographs the frayed tuft and the card tier inherits the silhouette for free. The price is one identical fray on all 26,647 tufts, which per-instance yaw, mirrored `u` on alternate cards and the 0.5–1.5 m size range are what keep from reading as a stamp.

**Grass is where the thinning stops being an optimisation and becomes the feature.** Trees hold full density to 80 m because they can; grass cannot, because instance count is linear in that radius in both terms of `πF²D + 2πFD(R−F)` and F = 80 at 3/m² is 100,000 tufts. F = 20 m for the clump carpet -- exactly its last mesh band, so nothing carrying real geometry is ever thinned -- gives 27k instances for a 70 m horizon where a hard disc would need 46k, and past it the bed is 1.5 tufts/m² at 40 m and 0.86 at 70. The dither dissolve is what makes that invisible: each tuft carries its own `fullRadius / rank` cull distance, so the thinning bands fade instead of stepping. **Tiles are 8 m rather than the tree scatter's 25**, because the keep-fraction is evaluated once per tile from its nearest corner and a tile wide relative to F over-keeps its whole far half -- and because 25 m at this density is 1,875 candidates in one build job, which is a visible hitch.

**The tile keep is a conservative superset of the rim's exact law, and hiding is what makes that free.** A tile is thinned once from its nearest corner, so a tuft on its far edge is kept as though it stood a tile-diagonal closer; the per-instance rim, which is exact, then takes it away anyway. 7,441 of 26,647 instances are resident and past their own trigger. What that buys is the right picture -- visible density is the smooth `F/d` law with no step at any tile boundary -- and what it *would* cost is instances that transform and discard every frame. So they are set invisible: 7,250 of them, the rest sitting inside the slack band below, for **23.4% of the grass bill** (62.0k resident, 47.5k drawn). They stay **resident**, because they are precisely the tufts that appear as the player walks toward them: re-showing one is a byte where regrowing the tile is a job. **The cut used to be at zero opacity and now it is at the trigger**, which is a real change and not a restatement: `fade == 0` was provably invisible but only existed because the dissolve was a distance ramp, and the ramp is what had to go. What replaces the proof is a measurement plus a floor -- no tuft's gone-distance is under `FULL_RADIUS`, so nothing can be hidden inside `FULL_RADIUS * RIM_AT` = 18.5 m, and `check-grass` finds the nearest hidden tuft at 18.99 m and 18 of 26,647 still on a mesh tier out in that last metre and a half.

**The sweep's only failure mode is being late to *show*, and it is bought off with slack rather than with frequency.** Sweeping 17k far instances every frame is 0.088 ms, cheap enough to pay outright, but it is amortised over 8 frames anyway -- 0.011 ms -- with each tile's phase derived from its *coordinates*, not its position in the tile Map, since Map order changes on every evict and admit. Being late to hide costs only the triangles the sweep was there to save; being late to show is a hole in the carpet. So a tuft is hidden only once it is a slack margin past its own trigger, sized to how far the camera travels in one sweep from a decaying peak-speed estimate, and a rising slack forces an immediate sweep. The show test sits `RIM_HYST` = 2 m inside the hide test, so what `check-grass` gates is that no hidden tuft is ever deeper than that inside its own trigger -- worst 1.72 m at every speed it tries, including a standing start and a ramp to 30 m/s, which is the gate, because nothing in a still frame shows this.

**The card bed is being traded for opaque blade geometry, because 81% of its fragment work is discarded.** The bed draws **20.6 full eyes of alpha-tested fragments per eye per frame** against a tuft texture that is **18.9% opaque**, and the `discard` that throws the other 81% away also disables low-resolution-Z for the whole draw on a tiled Adreno, so the occluded layers are shaded before they are rejected too. `src/props/grass-blades.js` is the other end of the trade: a clump is ten opaque untextured triangles, one per blade, covering only the area that used to be opaque -- ~3.9 eyes rather than 20.6, with LRZ back on. It also drops the stereo mismatch, the fawning, the cutout silhouette and the edge-on wasted instance, all four of which are the card's and not the grass's. **The bill moves from fill to triangles**, which is the budget this headset has room in: the shipped disc at 8 clumps/m², full to 6.5 m and culled at 30 is ~4,500 clumps and 24k triangles. `/gen-grass` previews it against the real terrain, LOD and snow layers with the model and the density law both on sliders. **Matching a blade's foot to the ground needed more than `shade()`, and that is worth knowing wherever a prop is tinted to the terrain.** `shade()` is the chunk mesher's VERTEX tint; the colour actually drawn is that tint carried through the fragment chain in `terrain/terrain-material.js`, and the last stage alone -- `uGrassTone`, (0.45, 0.92, 0.45) -- halves red and blue on every green fragment in the world. A clump painted with `shade()` therefore reads grey and waxy against the meadow it stands in, and flat besides, since the 1 km region layer and the 137 m mottle are what give the ground its variation from one clump to the next. `terrainTint` in `src/gen-grass-main.js` replays those three stages on the CPU, per clump, reading the amplitudes and the palette live off the terrain material's own uniforms so a retune of the ground retunes the grass with it -- 3.1 ms per 20,000 clumps. The ground photograph is skipped because it is divided by its own mean and so preserves one, and the grit layers because they are finer than a clump. **Getting the tint right is only half of it: a base colour matches the ground only if it is LIT like the ground**, and two things stood between it and that. Three's double-sided path flips the normal toward the viewer (`normal *= faceDirection` in `normal_fragment_begin`), which is right for a solid seen from inside and turns every back-facing blade black -- the same trap `src/material.js` documents for leaf cards, and the same one-line undo. Then `normalUp`, the bend of the face normal toward vertical, has to go **all the way to 1**: the damage a residual tilt does is scaled by the SUN's elevation, not by the tilt, so 0.70 -- 20-25 degrees off vertical -- is invisible at noon (dotNL 0.58 to 0.98 across a clump) and splits neighbouring blades between black and fully lit at a 10-degree dawn (0.00 to 0.49). A world clock running an hour a minute spends most of its time in the second regime. At 1 the clump shades as one flat patch of ground and the tip ramp carries the relief instead; what is left is that a blade on a hillside is still lit as though the hillside were level, bounded by the 38-degree slope cap on placement. **What it costs is on-screen TRIANGLES and nothing else**, measured on the headset five ways: 0.116 ms per blade-per-clump straight through 5, 10 and 20 blades (~1.13 ms for the bed at ten); halving height AND width, a 4x cut in projected area, moved it by nothing; trading blade count against density is neutral in both directions, so there is no per-instance term; freezing the scatter's `update()` costs nothing, so it is not the CPU; and with the bed off-frustum the blade count stops mattering, so it is not vertex work either. Only the **2x2 shading quad** fits all five -- a triangle thinner than a pixel still shades a whole quad, so the far field pays four fragments for a fraction of one, and fill tracks blade COUNT rather than blade AREA. **A distance card was built against that floor and rejected on look.** Past 8 m a clump's ten triangles collapsed to the instance origin and one cylindrically-spun quad stood in their place, carrying a baked photograph of four clumps, cross-dissolved over ±0.75 m against complementary dither thresholds -- both rungs in a single geometry with the collapse done in the vertex stage, because an InstancedMesh holds exactly one geometry and that is the only ladder available to it. It took 37% of the bed's drawn triangles off the screen and it looked unacceptably bad in the headset: a legible band of billboards, flat, missing the near field's shading variation across the crossover. Carrying the per-clump tip ramp onto the card and narrowing the clump's disc so it photographed square rather than as a strip both helped and neither was enough. **The far field is thinned instead**, by the `(F/d)³` law alone. `design/attic/grass-distance-cards.md` has what was tried, and the objection that stood before it was built and stands now: a cylindrical card lies back at you when seen from the air, and this world is flown in.

**With grass running, /v2 sits at ~158k of the 350k ceiling** -- 45k terrain, 65k forest, 31k ferns and 17k grass. The forest is measured rather than estimated now: ~41,000 trees placed inside 1500 m on a flat headless world at standing eye height of which the rim dissolve holds back ~9,900, leaving 3.6k of it in the 8 LOD0 meshes inside 8 m, 23.4k in the 64 LOD1 meshes out to 22.5 m, 8.3k across the 1,380 crossed cards to 100 m and 29.6k in the 29,640 one-triangle billboards behind them -- down from 131k, ~39k of which came off the far tier when its card went to one triangle, and 17k more when the bundled LOD1 was withdrawn and the 313 trees it held became crossed cards in the same places at 6 triangles instead of 60. About 20k of that has since been spent back deliberately, taking the mesh out from 10 m to 22.5 m in two tiers so the trees a player stands under are geometry for longer. That leaves the far band at **46% of the forest's bill and 95% of its instances**, so what the forest costs is now essentially how many one-triangle cards stand inside 1500 m. **The five rock beds are not in that figure and have not been measured in a frame either**; the bank they draw from is 15,791 triangles in total, and the panel's `rocks` row carries the drawn count and triangles the five of them sum to. Strips are where 36k of that went: the same meadow drawn as clumps is 53k. The fern bed used to be the biggest prop layer at 99k; converting it to this thinned scatter took it to 62k and halving its density to 0.5/m² took it to 31k, so the layer with the most room left in it is now the forest.

**Why not a third mesh tier.** Compared at equal budget, a third tier does push real geometry from 151 m out to 234 m. But what it puts there is a 45-triangle conifer at 94 px with no needles and no silhouette, replacing a 128 px impostor of the real canopy whose only defect -- parallax -- is already under 2° at that range, and every tier withdrawn from in front of it makes that trade worse rather than better: what a third mesh would displace is a 6-triangle cross at 22.5-100 m and a single triangle past that, so mesh out to 234 m buys the worse picture at 7.5x or 45x what it replaces. The third tier spends a geometry slot, a build step and a fourth band crossing to install a *worse* representation.

**The cheap canopy WAS built, shipped, and then withdrawn on looks, and what replaced it saves a third as much on purpose.** The first LOD1's crown was a bundle of twenty big triangles thrown through the tree, tiling the species' leaf-spray texture at the spray's true world size, so the canopy read off the texture at world scale rather than off the mesh. Per species at the base size, LOD0 → LOD1: pine 792 → 83, oak 516 → 47, birch 350 → 49, aspen 432 → 51, each exactly trunk 3 + limbs x 1 + bundle 20 -- 7 to 12 times cheaper than LOD0 and nested inside its silhouette to a few percent. None of that was enough: over the 10-45 m band it held, twenty blades read as blades close up and as a worse cross further off. **Cheap and well-nested is not the same as convincing**, and no triangle argument could have caught it.

**The tier that came back leaves the crown alone and coarsens only the WOOD.** `treeLod(p, 1)` is now three parameters -- `trunkSides` 3, `branchSides` 1, the latter turning each limb's barrel into a single upright fin, and `roots` 0, which deletes the LOD0-only root crown -- and nothing else, so the cards are the identical cards in the identical seats. Per species: pine 819 → 547, oak 543 → 415, birch 377 → 241, aspen 459 → 315; across the four the mean goes 550 → 380, a 31% cut running 1.31x on a big oak (few fat limbs) to 1.56x on a birch (whose trunk and roots are most of its bill). **The nesting is now exact rather than close**: height and crown width agree to every digit over 256 builds and the foliage matches vertex for vertex, where the bundled tier was 1% narrow on oak and 6% on pine. The honest cost is that a limb at 8-22.5 m is roughly 7-19 px wide on a Quest 2 panel, so replacing a 5-sided cone with a flat fin is a real if small cue loss; `addFin` orients the fin with the UP-ish perpendicular so a ground-level view looks across it rather than along its edge.

**The near field's quality knob is now two numbers, and they are priced very differently.** A mesh tree costs 550 triangles at LOD0 (10 of them the root crown) and 380 at LOD1 averaged over the bank, and the count grows as the SQUARE of the reach, so where the boundaries sit matters more than what the tiers cost. Measured: 8 trees inside 8 m at 3.6k, 64 more out to 22.5 m at 23.4k, **27.0k for 72 mesh trees where LOD0 alone out to 22.5 m costs 37.1k for the same 72**. The reach was 15 m and is 22.5, which is 39 more mesh trees for 14k more triangles at the measured spot and 17k at the densest of six -- bought to keep a crossed card from standing close enough to walk up to, and the reason the forest row moved from 51k to 65k.

**Density near the eye is the lever; draw distance is nearly free.** Measured for a 2 ferns/m² carpet with cards running to 510 m: the 25-510 m band is 0.15x an eye buffer of fill and, as `InstancedMesh`, zero per-frame CPU. The nearest 12 m is 1.42x -- **82% of the whole carpet's fill cost lives inside 12 metres**, and it scales linearly with near density. So the tuning order is: set near density against fill, then push the card distance out as far as it reads, because the far bands cost almost nothing.

**Card reach is set by pixels, and it is further out than it feels.** A 0.55 m fern on Quest 2's default eye buffer (~16.2 px/deg):

| Fern subtends | Distance |
| --- | --- |
| 20 px | 25 m |
| 10 px | 51 m |
| 5 px | 102 m |
| 3 px | 170 m |
| 1 px | 510 m |

So "cards until each is under 3 px" is **170 m**, not 500 m; 500 m is where a fern is one pixel. Both are far past where a card would previously have been culled, and the fill-rate numbers above say both are affordable.

**Clump cards are the tier that makes the far bands legal, and instance count is why.** At 2 ferns/m² with density halving per distance doubling, individual cards out to 510 m is **76,000 instances**. In `BatchedMesh` that is 2.8 ms/frame on desktop and ~11 ms on Quest 2, spent entirely on culling and sorting 2-triangle quads. Two independent fixes, and the ladder uses both:

| Ladder | Instances | Triangles | BatchedMesh CPU (Quest 2 est.) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| card per fern to 510 m | 76,000 | 208k | ~11.2 ms -- the whole frame |
| clumps of 8 past 170 m | 31,100 | 118k | ~4.6 ms |
| clumps of 8 past 60 m, 32 past 170 m | 11,800 | 79k | ~1.7 ms |

...and moving the far bands to tiled `InstancedMesh` takes that last column to **zero** regardless of which row you pick, at the cost of ~20 draw calls. The clumping still earns its place on triangles and on rebuild cost, and it looks better: one card baked from 8 ferns has the silhouette of a patch, and sparse individual cards at distance look like a comb-over.

**Beyond the card reach: bake the foliage into the terrain.** Use the same noise field that *would have* placed props to modulate the terrain material's albedo and normal (darker, greener, mottled). This is what shipped open-world games do, and it costs essentially nothing. It is the layer *under* the cards, not a replacement for them -- the terrain modulation runs everywhere including underfoot, and the cards sit on top of it out to wherever they stop being worth an instance.

**Why not a lower near density instead.** Because near density is the one thing you can actually see, and the fill-rate arithmetic above is all measured against 2 ferns/m² -- 1.42x an eye buffer inside 12 m, which is inside the ~2x Quest 2 sustains. **What /v2 actually ships is 0.5 ferns/m²**, a quarter of that, and both halvings were look calls rather than fill calls: with a 3 tuft/m² grass class underneath, 2/m² of fern reads as a wall and 1/m² as undergrowth you would have to push through, where the picture wanted was ferns growing in a meadow. So the near-field fill number is a quarter of the one the arithmetic above worries about -- and the caveat attached to it now bites for the other reason, since the fern bed is no longer the only thing contributing near-field overdraw. If fill rate binds on-device it still binds in that first 12 m, and the fix is fewer fronds per fern or a shorter grass class, not a shorter draw distance.

### Per Meta's WebXR best practices

- Sort opaque front-to-back
- **One real-time light maximum** (the sun/moon directional). Everything else is baked -- §8
- **No shadow map passes** -- they double draw calls
- KTX2/Basis texture compression throughout
- Stagger CPU work: sway, weather, and animation logic at 30 Hz while rendering at 72 Hz
- Render alpha-tested foliage *after* all opaque geometry
