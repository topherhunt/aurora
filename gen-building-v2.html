the warp field

One displacement field over space, applied to the finished vertex array. Because it is keyed on POSITION, two vertices that were coincident get the same answer and the shell stays airtight -- which is why the crookedness can be this large without opening a seam. Set strength to 0 to see the straight building underneath it.

Normals are rebuilt afterwards, and the same pass decides which edges are hard. Smoothing is a per-VERTEX decision made in the geometry -- not per material and not per object -- so it costs no triangles and no memory: every quad already owns its own four corners, and the pass only overwrites numbers in the normal array. Timber gets averaged normals so a five-sided log lights as a cylinder; stone, plaster and roof coverings do not, because their broken arrises are the point. smooth is the crease angle between the two.

what the plan decided

LOD by re-generation

Not decimation. The collapse decimator refuses to collapse an edge that borders a hole, and a building is holes by construction. So the far tiers are the same plan built with less of it -- detail 1 drops the bevelling, the rounding and the 3D joinery, and every window, frame and shutter becomes one flat rectangle. Detail 0 is a box per mass, a roof of four triangles with no overhang at all, and one rectangle for each window and the door: at that range an eave is a dark pixel and the pattern of openings is the only thing separating a building from a crate. Every tier is warped by the same field, so the silhouette does not pop on the swap.

the card it becomes

Past 200 m a building is flat, and it stays a card the rest of the way out. §5's rule is that a card's failure to turn stays under 2° beyond depth × 28.6, and the kit's narrow axis measures 5.8 m median. What stands there is one photograph on two planes at 90°, four triangles, out to 1.5 km, where a 6 m ridge is 3.7 px and three specks on a far hillside are the difference between an inhabited landscape and an empty one.

The two planes keep their own true widths, so a longhouse is still 21 m broadside and 5 m end-on -- the silhouette is right from every angle, and each plane stretches the picture to fit. What is shared is the picture, and it is shared much further than one building: the bank holds twenty photographs, one per wall style × roof kind, each of a plain stand-in cottage, and every building wearing that material pair borrows it. The cottage is the one kind allowed all five styles and all four roofs, and the four kinds' legal grids union to exactly those twenty, so nothing in the world is left without a picture. Twenty slices is 1.25 MB, against 9.5 for one photograph per variant and 19 for two.

What is given up is proportion, not material: an inn carries its eaves at 0.53 of ridge height and the cottage standing in for it at 0.46, so the eave line lands about 3 px out of a silhouette 28 px tall at the near edge of the band, and 0.4 px at the far one. The narrow plane also shows a flank where it should show a gable, but at 200 m it is ten pixels across. There is no spun billboard: a town is thirty buildings, so the two triangles it saves are sixty across the village, and one width shown from every angle is a 4× error on a longhouse. Pick cross and it draws beside the detail-0 mesh it stops matching; turn on gallery to see twelve seeds sharing twenty pictures.

the tiles it wears

Shared with v1, unchanged: nine layers of one 128² array plus two decal sheets. UVs are always taken from the unwarped geometry, so the warp bends the shape without smearing the tile. Walls measure them off world extents ÷ TILE_METRES -- absolute world height is what makes a log course run level round a corner instead of stepping at it -- while anything that is a discrete object rather than a piece of surface (a pane, a shutter leaf, a window's flat far-tier stand-in) gets whole tiles fitted to its own rectangle instead.
