the LOD ladder

Every tier is the same swept surface sampled on fewer sides and fewer rings. The radius is a pure function of (distance along the spine, angle around it), so a coarser tier asks that function for less and gets a plainer answer -- a tier change loses facets, it does not swap in a different log. That is why a snag is a far better LOD candidate than a rock: its silhouette is a near-cylinder, and a cylinder simplifies gracefully -- drop a rock from 12 sides to 6 and the outline develops corners nothing in nature has, drop a trunk and it stays a trunk. Switch distance assumes each triangle should stay above ~3 px on a Quest 2 eye buffer (16.2 px/°), the same model the rock bench uses.

the §5 budget

DESIGN.md §5's prop table has no row for dead wood; it files stumps and logs under bush, at 84 / 56 / 28 triangles for LOD0/1/2. This table holds the three tiers against those numbers. The row was written before this generator existed -- it was a guess at what a prop of this size costs, not a measurement of this one -- so a tier that runs over is a conversation, not automatically a bug. What it cannot be is an accident: if LOD0 wants 96 triangles because the break needs its jagged ring, that is a case somebody made, and §5 gets edited to say so.

moss and snow

Neither costs a triangle, and neither is a texture this page ships. Both are the one shared prop material's global uniforms (src/material.js), selected by TEXTURE LAYER -- so dead wood grows moss and catches snow because its bark and heartwood layers are named in MOSS_LAYERS and SNOW_WOOD_LAYERS, and for no other reason. Moss is a real second atlas fetch (LAYER.MOSS) because moss is nothing but grain: tint the bark green and you get green bark. It also blends rather than cutting over -- a colony thins into the wood across several centimetres, where snow has a genuine one-pixel rim and is drawn with one. Snow leans on which way a surface faces about twice as hard here as it does on foliage, which is the whole reason a fallen log whitens along its top first instead of frosting evenly. And moss carries a height cue measured from the instance's own root, not from world Y -- which is why a snag is green at the foot and clean at the break, while a log, whose whole length sits at root height, is mossy end to end.
